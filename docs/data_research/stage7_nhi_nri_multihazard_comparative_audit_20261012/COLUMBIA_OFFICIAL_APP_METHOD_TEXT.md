> EXPLORATORY MULTI-HAZARD INDEX AUDIT — NOT FORMAL STAGE 7

Coastal Flood This layer depicts the likelihood of hazardous coastal flooding in waterfront areas throughout the U.S. It includes measures for high tide flooding, hurricane storm surge, and sea level rise scenarios for 1-3 feet. The data values represent a composite coastal flood hazard score based on the summation of hazard scores from high tide flooding, hurricane storm surge, and sea-level rise scenarios for 1-3 feet. Classification Very low: 1.0-2.0 Low: 2.1-3.0 Medium: 3.1-4.0 High: 4.1-6.0 Very High: 6.1-7.0 Source: NOAA Coastal Flood Exposure Mapper | https://coast.noaa.gov/digitalcoast/tools/flood-exposure.html Limitations: This dataset currently excludes Alaska due to source data coverage. Drought This layer presents the U.S. Drought Monitor’s weekly drought severity across the last twenty-two years (2000-2022). The data values represent the average weekly drought severity index recorded for each census tract between 2000 and 2022. Classification Very low: 0-0.28 Low: 0.29-.67 Medium: .68-1.10 High: 1.11-1.50 Very High: 1.51-2.46 Source: National Drought Mitigation Center/USDA/NOAA | https://droughtmonitor.unl.edu/Data.aspx Limitations: The dataset is limited to droughts occurring over the past 22 years. Additionally, the duration of each historic drought is not factored into this indicator at this time. Earthquakes This layer represents the USGS’ “Percent chance of damaging shaking”, which represents the probability of the occurrence of a damaging earthquake in 100 years. It utilizes a Modified Mercalli Intensity Scale of MMI VI (slight damage shaking) and loose ground soil conditions of D classification (260 m/s). Includes ground motion amplification using topographic based ("site specific") Vs30 values. This measure is a shift from the more commonly used ground motion maps (peak ground acceleration exceedance probability in 50 years maps). The data values represent the percent chance of damaging earthquake shaking in 100 years, with shaking equivalent to the Modified Mercalli Intensity VI. The higher the value, the higher likelihood of there being an earthquake that surpasses a Mercalli intensity of VI. Classification Very low: 0.0-9.5% Low: 9.6-28.8% Medium: 28.9-59.6% High: 59.7-86.6% Very High: 86.7-100% Source: USGS | CONUS: https://www.sciencebase.gov/catalog/item/5d55d0a5e4b01d82ce8eafa9 | Hawaii: https://www.sciencebase.gov/catalog/item/60be1beed34e86b9389104a1 Limitations: Data for Alaska and Puerto Rico was not available at the time of publishing, but USGS is expected to release data for both territories in the near future. Additionally, the assumption is made that everyone lives on class D soil (vs 260 m/s) which could over or under state risk depending on local soil conditions and building codes. Extreme Heat This layer represents the level of heat hazard based on the number of extreme heat days (based on the Heat Index) that occurred from January 1st, 2000, to December 31st, 2021. The number of extreme heat days, as opposed to the number of heat waves, accounts for extreme heat events that occur for only one day and for abnormally warm days during historically cooler months in addition to warmer months. The data values indicate the average number of days per year between 2000 and 2021, in which the heat index surpassed the 95th percentile, above the average number of days the heat index surpassed the 95th percentile between 1980 and 1999. Classification Very low: 0-615 extreme heat days (0-29 days per year) Low: 616-753 extreme heat days per year (29-35 days per year) Medium: 754-947 extreme heat days per year (36-45 days per year) High: 948-1540 extreme heat days per year (45-73 per year) Very High: 1541-3014 extreme heat days per year (73-143 days per year) Source: DAYMET/Oak Ridge National Laboratories | https://daymet.ornl.gov/ Limitations: The indicator derived from the dataset does not represent heat waves, or consecutive days of extreme heat, as there is no agreed upon convention of a “heat wave”. Additionally, the heat Index calculation used here requires the use of vapor pressure as a proxy for humidity when using the DAYMET data source. The use of weather station data may also underestimate heat island effects in some urban areas. Floods The layer depicts areas with significant, moderate, and low flood risk as defined by the Federal Emergency Management Agency. The flood zones indicate the likelihood of flooding, including 1% chance of flooding in a given year over a 30-year mortgage, 0.2% chance of annual flooding, or the possibility of flooding with the exact hazard undetermined. The data values correspond to the weighted probability of a flood event occurring over various periods of time, including 1% annual flood chance, 0.2% annual flood chance, and an unknown but possible risk of flooding. Classification Very low: 0-30.04 Low: 30.05-81.70 Medium: 81.71-115.18 High: 115.19-151.43 Very High: 151.44-300.0 Source: FEMA | https://www.fema.gov/flood-maps/national-flood-hazard-layer Limitations: A main limitation of this dataset is the significant gaps in the dataset coverage where flood hazard data are missing from the source data. To fill these gaps, data from FEMA’s “Pending” and “Preliminary” datasets were incorporated into the “Effective” layer. The data is also limited to FEMA’s NFHL dataset which is tied to insurable areas (FIRMs) for the National Flood Insurance Program, rather than a national full coverage gridded dataset. Additionally, the dataset does not include flash flood hazard or other flooding due to impervious surfaces. Hail This layer depicts the likelihood for extreme hail events, based on historical hail storm data for all recorded hail events across the U.S. and territories from 1955 to 2020. The data values represent the total number of hail events recorded in each census tract from 1955 to 2020. Classification Very low: 1-14 Low: 14.1-48 Medium: 48.1-119 High: 119.1-246 Very High: 246.1-583 Source: NOAA Storm Prediction Center | https://www.spc.noaa.gov/wcm/#stats Limitations: The dataset is created from a variety of local weather jurisdictions, suggesting that there is a possible data bias towards more populated areas. Hailstorm characteristics such as duration, intensity, size, or damage are also not accounted for in the current indicator. The Storm Prediction Center that aggregates the data also acknowledges that the data may not accurately reflect all hailstorm events. Hurricanes This layer represents the Saffir-Simpson Category 1 tropical cyclone wind speed (10-minute average sustained wind speeds of 64.8 mph or 29 m/s) return periods (number of years). The data values are the average return period within each census tract for experiencing a Category 1 wind speed hurricane. A return period is the estimated average time between events and is the inverse of the average frequency of occurrence. For example, a return period of 100 years can also be interpreted as 1/100 or 1% in a given year. The values are based on a synthetic dataset of 10,000 years of tropical cyclone data, which was derived from 38 years of IBTrACS data which underwent 1,000 realizations to create a 10 km resolution dataset. Classification Very low: 1001-10,693 years (0.09% - 0.00.01% 09% probability of occurrence in a given year) Low: 501-1000 years (0.19% - 0.10% probability of occurrence in a given year) Medium: 101-500 years (0.99% - 0.20% probability of occurrence in a given year) High: 51-100 years (1.96% - 1.00% -6probability of occurrence in a given year) Very High: 0-50 years (13.5% -2.00% probability of occurrence in a given year) Source: STORM Database / 4TU Centre for Research Data | https://data.4tu.nl/articles/dataset/STORM_tropical_cyclone_wind_speed_return_periods/12705164 Limitations: This dataset currently does not include data for tropical cyclones in the Eastern Pacific basin, although data exist and will be incorporated in future updates, and therefore excludes HI, AK, the West coast. Given that the STORM model used in this layer considers 38 years of hurricane data processed through simulations to estimate 10,000 years of cyclone occurrences, it may not reflect multi-decade variability over longer periods of time. It is also worth noting that as with all hurricane models reviewed, there is significantly less confidence in cyclone tracks after the storm moves further inland. Moreover, STORM and other hurricane models do not account for rainfall precipitation or storm surge associated with a tropical cyclone. Currently, no publicly available models exist which include all three factors. Lastly, the STORM model is built with present day climate conditions. Landslides This layer represents the susceptibility to rain-induced landslides. Landslide susceptibility values are calculated from several inputs that include ground slope, topography, forest cover change, seismicity, and distance to road networks. The output represents the “possibility” of a landslide within that area, with susceptibility values classified into five categories: very low, low, medium, high, and very high susceptibility to rain-induced landslides. The classification scheme was designed so that each risk category is roughly twice as large as the next classification, so the low designation is roughly twice as large as the medium designation. “This classification scheme was designed so that each category was twice as large as the next highest, e.g., the very low category contains roughly twice as many pixels as the low category. The decreasing category sizes should enable the user to focus efforts upon the most susceptible areas.” Classification Very low: 0.0-1.27 Low: 1.28-1.77 Medium: 1.78-2.42 High: 2.43-3.39 Very High: 3.40-5.0 Source: NASA | https://gpm.nasa.gov/landslides/coolrdata.html Limitations: This dataset is specifically modeled for rain-induced landslides, and as such does not account for earthquake-induced landslides. Additionally, the spatial resolution of the dataset is limited to approximately one kilometer, which may not account for terrain variability within that spatial unit. The model also incorporates data on landslide inventories, which are known to be biased towards areas with greater population density. Tornado This layer depicts the likelihood of tornadoes across the U.S., based on historical data for all tornadoes in the U.S. between 1950 to 2020. Not displayed values include tornado severity measures, including the mean EF value for each census tract. The data values represent the average of number of tornadoes by tornado path swath summed in each census tract between 1950 and 2020. Classification Very low: 1-3 tornadoes Low: 4-8 tornadoes Medium: 9-18 tornadoes High: 19-37 tornadoes Very High: 38-124 tornadoes Source: NOAA National Weather Service Storm Prediction Center | https://www.spc.noaa.gov/wcm/#stats Limitations: This dataset is created from a variety of local weather jurisdictions, suggesting a possible data bias towards more populated areas. Additionally, characteristics of tornado duration, intensity (EF Scale), or damage are also not accounted for in this indicator. Tsunami This layer presents pre-processed tsunami inundation zone from individual state maps (California, Washington, Oregon, and the East Coast). These have been rasterized and categorized by NOAA with a binary variable. This process was modeled after NOAA’s Coastal Flood Hazard Layer. The data values for tsunami hazard represent a binary variable indicating the existence or non-existence of tsunami hazard. Classification Tsunami Hazard Exists Tsunami Hazard Does Not Exist Source: NOAA Coastal Flood Hazard Composite | https://coast.noaa.gov/digitalcoast/tools/flood-exposure.html Limitations: This indicator depicts a binary hazard indicator (i.e. no tsunami hazard vs. tsunami hazard) and thus lacks a gradient of the hazard, which is important in distinguishing the scope of the tsunami threat (i.e., the West coast has a greater tsunami threat than the East coast, but they are given equal weight in this product). Additionally, the indicator here relies on 4 different data providers, which might process their data differently and may make different assumptions. Volcano This layer presents data from the volcano hazard assessment from USGS updated in 2018. The hazard scores utilized vary due to the differences in potentially active volcanoes’ eruptive style and geographic location. This layer creates a 30 km buffer for pyroclastic events around each potentially active volcano in the United States and its territories, and a 100 km buffer around each potentially active volcano for Tephra (ash etc.). USGS Hazard Threat scores (non-exposure) were summed to account for some census tracts being exposed to more than one volcano. A value of 1 was applied to the 100 km tephra radius. The data values represent the cumulative (summative) hazard score within each census tract based on USGS 2018 hazard assessments based on the potential for pyroclastic flow, and ash buffers. Classification Very low: 1.0-2.5 Low: 2.6-6.0 Medium: 6.1-12.0 High: 12.1-21.0 Very High: 21.1-35.0 Source: U.S. Geological Survey | https://pubs.er.usgs.gov/publication/sir20185140 Limitations: This dataset includes a 30 km buffer for proximate hazard and the 100 km buffer for tephra (ash), which are broad approximations of the real threat that could result from volcano eruptions. These are the best available generalizable approximations for the US. It’s important to note that areas with overlapping buffers from multiple volcanoes receive a simple summed hazard score (i.e. a census tract may be exposed to more than one volcano), which may over- or under-estimate the hazard. Lastly, USGS does provide a separate hazard score of human impact, the exposure score, which is currently excluded from this product to stay in line with the other hazard layers included. Wildfires This layer represents Risk to Potential Structures (RPS). A measure that integrates annual wildfire likelihood (probability) and intensity with generalized consequences to a home based on a response curve, which is a function of the potential flame lengths that can occur in a certain location. For every place on the landscape, it poses the hypothetical question, “What would be the relative risk to a house if one existed here?”. The data values of Risk to Potential Structures represent a dimensionless relative index of wildfire risk to a home (or other structure) if one were present. It is relative in that a doubling of the value indicates a doubling of the overall risk. Classification Very low: 0.0-.269 Low: .270-1.013 Medium: 1.014-2.444 High: 2.445-4.876 Very High: 4.877-11.938 Source: United States Department of Agriculture, Forest Service | https://www.fs.usda.gov/rds/archive/Catalog/RDS-2020-0016 Limitations: In general, there is no best fire hazard model that perfectly approximates real life conditions. The source dataset chosen is utilized by USDA in their Wildfire Risk to Communities product, which is utilized by many entities across the US. However, this model does not factor in mitigation efforts at the local level. In addition, wildfire fuel conditions, or land cover, used in this data source are from 2014 (LANDFIRE 2014) and likely outdated (although an update to this data source is expected this year (2023)). Lastly, this dataset currently excludes Puerto Rico. Wind This layer depicts the incidence of damaging wind events, based on historical wind storm data across the U.S. between 1955 and 2020. The data represents the total number of damaging wind events recorded from 1955 to 2020 in a given census tract. Classification Very low: 1-9 wind events Low: 10-26 wind events Medium: 27-53 wind events High: 54-114 wind events Very High: 114-402 wind events Source: NOAA National Weather Service Storm Prediction Center | https://www.spc.noaa.gov/wcm/#stats Limitations: This dataset was created from a variety of local weather jurisdictions, suggesting a data bias towards more populated areas. Windstorm characteristics such as duration, intensity, size, or damage are also not accounted for in the current indicator. The Storm Prediction Center that aggregates the data also acknowledges that the data may not accurately reflect all hailstorm events. Winter Weather This layer represents the hazard of severe winter weather, including snowstorms, blizzards, extreme cold, and other winter weather, based on historical weather events since 1950. The total number of winter weather events recorded in the county since 1950, including Blizzards, Cold/Wind Chill, Extreme Cold/Wind Chill, Freeze, Heavy Snow, Ice Storm, Winter Storm, and Winter Weather. Data was then interpolated to estimate the hazard risk for counties that lacked sufficient data. Classification Very low: 0-33.3 events Low: 33.4-69.2 events Medium: 69.3-107.1 events High: 107.2-158.9 events Very High: 159.0-404.9 events Source: NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information | https://www.ncdc.noaa.gov/stormevents/ Limitations: Although the dataset includes events back to 1950, most winter storm events in this source were only recorded and included after 1996. Additionally, because the National Weather Service aggregates data from a variety of sources (including county, state, and federal emergency workers, as well as newspaper articles and the general public) the storm data is likely biased towards more heavily populated areas. The location data attached to some storm events is imprecise or unreliable because it lacks specific latitude and longitude coordinates. Therefore, events were cross-walked to the county that they were reported to have occurred in. Due to the data bias towards populated areas, some areas of the country that are less populated (such as Alaska) had significant data gaps that may not reflect the historical occurrence of winter weather events in those areas. To help address this issue, the data was interpolated to fill missing gaps by incorporating storm event data from neighboring counties. These spatial interpolation methods (Kriging) may have introduced some error in the data.
