## P0001 / body block 1 | Front matter

From Substation Damage to Community Recovery: Tract-Level Assessment of Post-Earthquake Power-Service Restoration in Los Angeles


## P0003 / body block 3 | Front matter

Yinchen Yi, Yutong Li, Marta C. González


## P0004 / body block 4 | Front matter

Abstract


## P0005 / body block 5 | Front matter

Major earthquakes can disrupt electric-power systems in multi-facted aspects. In metropolitan regions, post-earthquake power recovery depends not only on which transmission and substation assets are damaged, but also on how restoration resources are dispatched and which communities remain without service. This study presents a tract-level framework for evaluating post-earthquake electric-power service disruption and recovery in the Los Angeles region. The framework reconstructs a source-gated transmission/substation topology, simulates scenario-based substation damage using fragility functions, translates substation functionality into census-tract service availability through tract–substation dependency weights, and compares unconstrained recovery with logistics-aware restoration strategies including rule-based priorities, and Genetic Algorithm (GA)-based scheduling recommendations. Using the 2%-in-50-year seismic scenario as the primary case, the results show that network vulnerability is concentrated in a limited set of high-impact substations and that tract-level recovery is spatially uneven even before crew-dispatch constraints are introduced. Under logistics-aware restoration, strategy performance cannot be explained by network centrality alone, as population exposure, tract-substation dependency, and repair sequencing jointly shape service recovery. GA-based schedules provide the fastest milestone recovery among the tested strategies, while population-impact and hospital-prioritized rules provide competitive non-optimization alternatives. The recovery-vulnerability typology further shows that delayed modeled restoration and social vulnerability are not perfectly aligned. A composite hotspot analysis identifies census tracts where slow recovery, social vulnerability, older housing stock, and disaster-risk exposure coincide. The proposed framework supports risk-informed restoration planning by comparing recovery priorities and identifying communities where post-earthquake power-service delays may raise greater equity concerns.

## P0008 / body block 8 | Front matter

Introduction


## P0009 / body block 9 | Front matter

Power and electric systems serve as the backbone for the normal operation of society. As key components, the functionality of substations and transmission lines is ciritical for the overall supply and service of electricity. When earthquakes damage the electric-power system, neighborhoods can experience different levels and durations of service disruption because local electricity access depends on which substations remain functional, how transmission connectivity is affected, and how quickly damaged assets can be restored. Post-earthquake power recovery is therefore not only a system-performance problem, but also a community-service problem, as electricity supports emergency response, healthcare operation and communication in stricken regions. Recent resilience research has emphasized that infrastructure recovery should not be evaluated only through aggregate system functionality, because service losses and restoration delays are often spatially uneven and socially differentiated. This perspective is particularly relevant for Los Angeles, where seismic hazard, power service, population density, and social vulnerability overlap unevenly across space.

## P0011 / body block 11 | 1.1 Equity-aware infrastructure restoration

Recent infrastructure resilience studies have increasingly shifted from system-centered performance metrics toward community-centered recovery outcomes. Logan and Guikema (2020) reframed community resilience around equitable access to essential services, emphasizing that recovery should be evaluated not only by infrastructure functionality, but also by residents’ spatially uneven access to services such as food, health care, education, water, power, sanitation, and communications. Dhakal and Zhang (2022) incorporated social equity and vulnerability into infrastructure resilience assessment by accounting for unequal distributions of functional loss and recovery time, thereby linking resilience metrics to both inequality and impacts on socially vulnerable communities. Ding et al. (2026) extended this line of work to the census-tract scale by examining the spatial-temporal dynamics of public infrastructure service inequality during hurricanes using 311 service-request data, showing that reported service requests and resolution time vary systematically across geographic exposure, social vulnerability, and disaster-risk groups. In the power-outage context, Loni and Asadi (2024) developed a data-driven index of social vulnerability to prolonged outages in California, while Lin et al. (2022) framed equity as a missing dimension in power-grid resilience and argued that recognitional, procedural, and distributional equity should be embedded into power-system planning and disaster management. Esmalian et al. (2022) further showed, through a hazard-household-infrastructure multi-agent model, that infrastructure service disruptions can impose uneven household-level hardship, highlighting the need to evaluate recovery outcomes across socially differentiated populations.


## P0012 / body block 12 | 1.1 Equity-aware infrastructure restoration

Beyond assessment, a related body of work has incorporated community characteristics directly into restoration planning and resource-allocation models. Chen et al. (2025) advanced community-centered infrastructure restoration by combining population, social vulnerability, and public facilities such as hospitals and emergency shelters into restoration-priority weights for interdependent power and water infrastructure recovery. Li et al. (2023) proposed a data-driven and equity-centered framework for highway restoration following flood inundation, linking damage identification, restoration scheduling, and progress monitoring while considering social vulnerability in restoration prioritization. Chen et al. (2025) developed a human-centered infrastructure restoration framework that estimates post-disaster community demand using Bayesian updating and links demand uncertainty to restoration resource allocation for interdependent infrastructure systems. In power-grid resilience planning, Toplu-Tutay et al. (2024) incorporated equity metrics into a stochastic substation-hardening and power-flow optimization framework, approximating community-level outage impacts through census-tract-level vulnerability and well-being-loss measures.


## P0013 / body block 13 | 1.1 Equity-aware infrastructure restoration

Together, these studies show that a growing body of disaster recovery research has moved beyond purely technical system restoration and has begun to account for social vulnerability, essential-service access, and equity-aware resource allocation. This literature provides the conceptual and methodological basis for evaluating restoration outcomes by asking not only how much infrastructure functionality is restored, but also which communities regain service, when they regain it, and how recovery benefits and burdens are distributed across space.

A parallel stream of research has developed repair-scheduling, routing, and restoration-optimization models for disrupted critical infrastructure systems. Xu et al. (2026) reviewed post-disaster repair-resource scheduling models and showed that past research  has matured around restoration objectives, scheduling and routing decisions, repair and system-operation constraints, uncertainty treatment, and solution algorithms. Within this stream, Li et al. (2019) developed a joint optimization model that simultaneously assigns restoration tasks to multiple teams, schedules component repairs, and routes crews among geographically distributed damaged components while evaluating recovery performance using maximal network flow and unmet demand. Loggins et al. (2019) developed the CRISIS decision-support model to schedule repairs of damaged civil infrastructure according to stakeholder-defined priorities for dependent social infrastructure services, explicitly representing interdependencies between civil systems and critical social functions. Related work has also expanded restoration scheduling beyond purely component-based repair decisions. Shahverdi et al. (2025) formulated a decision-support model that coordinates specialized repair crews across interdependent power, water, and transportation lifelines to prioritize critical societal services such as hospitals. Shahverdi et al. (2024) further modeled personnel as “human infrastructure,” emphasizing that critical services require not only functional buildings and supporting lifelines but also skilled personnel who can remain in the affected region and travel to their workplaces. Recent power-restoration models have also addressed uncertainty-aware and adaptive decision-making: Jiang et al. proposed a predict-then-optimize framework that combines repair-duration uncertainty prediction with equity-constrained power restoration sequencing, and Li et al. developed a post-earthquake electricity-gas restoration framework that jointly models damage-information collection and repair-vehicle routing under partial observability.

## P0017 / body block 17 | 1.2 Substation Fragility, Repair Scheduling, Lifeline Restoration, and Critical Service Restoration

Several studies are especially relevant to earthquake-induced electric-power risk and restoration in Los Angeles. Monte Carlo-based studies have linked earthquake ground motion, component fragility, and electric transmission-network performance through power-flow analysis. Shinozuka et al. (2007) evaluated seismic disruption of the Los Angeles power transmission network by sampling component damage under multiple earthquake scenarios and assessing resulting network performance, while Johnson et al. (2020) similarly used Monte Carlo simulation and power-flow analysis to estimate demand not served under seismic damage. Other scenario-based work has emphasized the representation of regional earthquake hazard and downstream outage consequences. Soleimani et al. (2021) developed a Los Angeles multi-hazard earthquake scenario selection method that preserves risk estimates for ground shaking, liquefaction, and surface fault rupture while reducing computational burden. Building on these scenarios, Cheng et al. (2024) quantified census-tract-level electric-power risk in Los Angeles by combining probabilistic earthquake and damage scenarios, HAZUS-based component damage, DC load-flow analysis, and tract-level load-shed allocation, showing that substation damage is a major driver of outage risk and that solar-plus-storage benefits may remain spatially and socially uneven. Other studies have modeled post-earthquake electric-power restoration processes in Los Angeles more directly. Cagnan et al. (2006) provided an early Los Angeles-specific benchmark for post-earthquake electric-power restoration by using discrete-event simulation to translate hazard-driven substation damage into restoration rapidity risk. Their results showed that outage duration can vary geographically across LADWP service areas and can be shaped as much by repair-team and material bottlenecks as by initial component damage. Xu et al. (2007) further formulated a stochastic optimization model for scheduling LADWP post-earthquake inspection, damage assessment, and repair tasks, using restoration curves and outage-duration metrics to compare optimized schedules with baseline or utility-informed restoration schedules. More broadly, logistics-oriented restoration studies such as Cavdaroglu et al. (2013) show that repair sequencing, crew assignment, travel time, precedence relationships, and repair uncertainty can substantially affect infrastructure recovery trajectories.

## P0020 / body block 20 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Despite these advances, a specific gap remains for earthquake-induced urban power-service recovery. Existing studies have advanced community-centered restoration, social-vulnerability-informed prioritization, repair-resource scheduling, power-system seismic risk assessment, and electrical-substation vulnerability modeling. However, these components are often treated at different analytical levels. Some studies focus on physical damage or load-shed risk, others on repair scheduling or restoration logistics, and still others on community vulnerability or service inequality. Relatively few studies integrate these engineering, logistics, and community-outcome components into a single metropolitan earthquake power-service recovery assessment that links physical damage, restoration operations, and tract-level recovery disparities. This gap limits the ability to evaluate not only how quickly the power system recovers, but also how physical damage, restoration logistics, and prioritization rules translate into uneven power-service recovery across census tracts and vulnerable communities.

To address these questions, this study develops a tract-level assessment of post-earthquake electric-power service disruption and recovery for the Los Angeles region. The assessment operationally couples scenario-specific seismic intensity, substation fragility, source-gated transmission/substation topology, tract-to-substation dependency mapping, damage-conditioned stochastic repair durations, road-network travel from repair-yard origins, alternative restoration-prioritization strategies, and consolidated metric-based tract typology identification. Recovery outcomes are evaluated using tract-level service metrics, including population-weighted recovery time and vulnerability-related recovery typologies derived from social, demographic, built-environment, and simulated recovery indicators. A sensitivity analysis further examines how assumptions on crew availability, repair-time scale, IDW dependency threshold, and source-gate threshold affect absolute recovery times and strategy-ranking stability. Integrative and operational, the study combines established hazard, fragility, network-dependency, repair-logistics, and vulnerability-assessment components into a transparent workflow for evaluating earthquake-specific power-service recovery and community-level recovery disparities.

Methodology


## P0028 / body block 28 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Fig. 1. Study Workflow


## P0029 / body block 29 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Fig. 1 summarizes the workflow used to evaluate post-earthquake electric-power service disruption and recovery in the Los Angeles region. The workflow begins with five groups of input data: transmission lines and substations, scenario PGA fields, roads and utility yards, hospitals, and tract-level attributes. These inputs are then processed through five analytical modules: topology and dependency construction, seismic damage simulation, damage-to-service translation, recovery modeling, and outputs interpretation. Together, the workflow translates scenario-specific substation damage into tract-level service recovery trajectories and recovery-vulnerability outcomes.


## P0030 / body block 30 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

First, a transmission/substation topology is constructed by combining substation point records with high-voltage transmission-line geometries. The resulting graph is used to represent network connectivity, identify source-connected components, and compute structural criticality measures such as degree, betweenness, and connectivity-related impact on the largest connected component. In parallel, a tract-to-substation dependency matrix is developed to allocate substation functionality to census tracts using topology-informed spatial proximity.


## P0031 / body block 31 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Second, seismic hazard intensity is assigned to each substation from scenario Peak Ground Acceleration (PGA) fields. The analysis considers three historical earthquake scenarios, the 1933 Long Beach, 1971 San Fernando, and 1994 Northridge events, together with a probabilistic 2%-in-50-year scenario. Lognormal fragility functions are then used in a Monte Carlo procedure to sample discrete substation damage states. These damage states are mapped to residual functionality levels and damage-conditioned stochastic restoration durations.


## P0032 / body block 32 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Third, substation damage is translated into tract-level service disruption through a damage-to-service propagation module. Residual substation functionality is routed through the source-gated transmission/substation topology and combined with tract-to-substation dependency weights to estimate tract-level power-service availability. This step provides the basis for the evaluation of how damaged substations and network accessibility affect census-tract service outcomes.


## P0033 / body block 33 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Fourth, recovery modeling is conducted under both an unconstrained baseline and a logistics-aware restoration setting. The unconstrained baseline characterizes tract-level recovery trajectories based on damage-state restoration durations without explicit crew-dispatch constraints. The logistics-aware restoration model incorporates utility yard origins, road-network travel times, and crew constraints. Within this model, recovery outcomes are compared across rule-based prioritization strategies and GA-based optimization benchmarks to evaluate how resource-constrained task sequencing affects tract-level service restoration.


## P0034 / body block 34 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Finally, recovery outcomes are interpreted using weighted recovery metrics, sensitivity analysis, and recovery-vulnerability typologies. Population- and vulnerability-weighted recovery trajectories are used to compare restoration strategies, while sensitivity analysis evaluates how major modeling assumptions affect recovery time and strategy-ranking stability. Simulated recovery indicators are then combined with social, demographic, built-environment, and hazard-related tract attributes. These features are standardized and fed into K-means clustering to identify census tracts where delayed recovery and vulnerability-related concerns coincide.


## P0035 / body block 35 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

The pipeline integrates heterogeneous geospatial datasets. The physical power grid is represented using substation inventories and high-voltage transmission-line geometries from California Energy Commission (CEC) and Homeland Infrastructure Foundation-Level Data sources. Scenario-specific ground-motion fields are obtained from United States Geological Survey (USGS) ShakeMap products. Census tract boundaries are obtained from U.S. Census Bureau TIGER/Line data, with population and demographic attributes joined from the American Community Survey. Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) and National Risk Index (NRI) attributes are linked at the tract level, and hospital locations are obtained from California Health and Human Services open data.

The study area covers the contiguous urbanized portion of Los Angeles County south of the San Gabriel Mountains, including the Los Angeles Basin, the San Fernando Valley, the San Gabriel Valley, and the Long Beach-Harbor area. This region represents the county’s primary continuous urban corridor, where dense population, critical facilities, and major electric-power infrastructure are concentrated across connected basin, valley, and coastal-plain settings.

## P0041 / body block 42 | 2.1.1 Topological Reconstruction of the Transmission Network

2.1.1 Topological Reconstruction of the Transmission Network


## P0042 / body block 43 | 2.1.1 Topological Reconstruction of the Transmission Network

Transforming raw GIS geometries into a mathematically valid network graph is a critical prerequisite for subsequent analysis. Transmission line records containing multiple geometric components are first decomposed into individual line-string components, which consist of several vertex pairs, so that each component could be processed consistently. To reconcile spatial offsets between the line and substation layers, the true endpoints of each line-string component are snapped to nearby substations. Endpoints within 150 m are directly assigned to the nearest substation, whereas endpoints within 150 to 375 m are assigned only when the nearest substation is clearly preferred over the second-nearest one, based on both an absolute distance margin test and a nearest to second-nearest distance ratio test. To preserve existing line-line connectivity, when a junction vertex cluster satisfies the snapping criteria, all vertices in the cluster are moved together to the corresponding nearby substation. Substations located near the interior of a line-string component are incorporated by splitting the line at the substation’s orthogonal projection point when the distance is within the specified tolerance, which is 10 m at default. The processed geometries were then converted into an undirected weighted physical graph Gphys=(Vphys,ℰphys), by treating line-string vertices as graph nodes and consecutive vertex pairs as weighted edges. To facilitate distance calculations, substations are mapped onto the nodes inside Gphyswith coordinates.


## P0043 / body block 44 | 2.1.1 Topological Reconstruction of the Transmission Network

Unlike the above mentioned physical weighted graph Gphys, the topological network G=(V,ℰ) is constructed by straight connections between each pair of directly-connected substations. A pair is considered directly adjacent only if the vertices listed in the shortest path in Gphys are not associated with any other substation. Undirected weighted edges are constructed with the weight being the Dijkstra calculated along-grid shortest path length in Gphys. The resulting graph G represents direct network adjacency among substations and is used for the subsequent topology and restoration analyses.

## P0223 / body block 227 | 2.6.2 K-means Clustering

As is shown in Fig. 2B, the topological network includes 92 nodes connected by 318 edges. As discussed, this topology is constructed by drawing direct links on the factual transmission network, which is shown in Fig. 2A. The average degree is 6.91, and the weighted average shortest-path length is 41.4 km, reflecting a moderately connected substation topology distributed over a spatially extensive urban region. The network is used for subsequent analyses of centrality, source connectivity, and recovery propagation.


## P0224 / body block 228 | 2.6.2 K-means Clustering

Substation importance depends on which centrality is considered. Degree centrality captures local connectivity, but a high degree does not reliably translate into system-wide consequence. Betweenness centrality highlights nodes that concentrate shortest-path routing and therefore represent transmission bottlenecks. Impact (λ2-based) centrality captures the contribution of a substation to global network fragmentation. Taken together, these centralities imply that restoration priorities should not be guided solely by substations with large number of transmission line connections.


## P0225 / body block 229 | 2.6.2 K-means Clustering

Notably, when the power grid fragments into disconnected sub-networks, transmission lines would no longer connect the available supply to demand across those sub-networks, so power outage can persist even if some local assets remain functional. The percolation curves in Fig. 2C track the network's largest connected component (LCC) fraction as substations are removed. Under random removal, connectivity decreases gradually, with only two more pronounced drops around the 33rd and 67th node, respectively.


## P0226 / body block 230 | 2.6.2 K-means Clustering

In contrast, targeted removal takes out nodes ranked by the orders of various centrality metrics calculated on the original graph, so each removal is going to represent a greater impact on the network structure than the random counterpart. As a result, the LCC fraction falls much faster. For example, within the first 35 degree-targeted removals, the LCC fraction drops to below 0.2, long before the random case reaches comparable degradation. This indicates that the largest connected component decomposes much faster under targeted removal, reflecting the concentrated impact on network connectivity from a small group of high-importance substations.


## P0227 / body block 231 | 2.6.2 K-means Clustering

This result indicates that the source-gated transmission topology is not uniformly vulnerable. Instead, network vulnerability is concentrated in a small group of substations whose disruption can fragment the transmission network more rapidly than random outages. This topological concentration provides the structural basis for later restoration-strategy comparisons, because repairing highly connected or high-impact substations can influence not only local service restoration but also the accessibility of source-connected network components.

3.5 Sensitivity Analysis


## P0252 / body block 257 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

This analysis is intended as a one-at-a-time sensitivity evaluation of major modeling assumptions. The tested ranges are grouped into two categories, namely restoration-related assumptions and topology-related assumptions. The restoration-related assumptions include crew availability and restoration time scaling, which directly affect repair capacity and damage-conditioned restoration duration. The topology-related assumptions include the IDW dependency threshold and source-gate threshold, which affect tract–substation dependency mapping and source-gated network accessibility. All sensitivity outcomes are evaluated relative to the baseline configuration.


## P0253 / body block 258 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Fig. 7 and Table 5 summarize the sensitivity of population-weighted recovery time T80pop to restoration-related and topology-related assumptions under the 2%-in-50-year scenario. The results show that population-weighted recovery milestones are primarily controlled by the restoration-related assumptions. Crew availability produces the largest within-strategy change in T80pop, with a maximum shift of 49.70 h across the tested range. This reflects the direct effect of crew capacity on parallel repair execution and queuing delays. Restoration time scaling produces a comparable maximum shift of 45.10 h, because it directly stretches or compresses the duration of damage-conditioned restoration tasks across the system.


## P0254 / body block 259 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

By contrast, topology-related parameters have smaller effects on aggregate T80pop. Varying the IDW dependency threshold changes how tract demand is distributed across substations, but its maximum effect on aggregate T80pop is only 1.10 h. This suggests that the population-weighted recovery milestone is relatively insensitive to moderate changes in weak tract–substation links after dependency weights are renormalized. The source-gate threshold has a larger but still moderate effect, with a maximum T80pop change of 5.70 h. This threshold affects which partially functional substations are allowed to participate in source-connected service propagation, but within the tested range it does not substantially alter the leading group of restoration strategies.


## P0255 / body block 260 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The sensitivity results also distinguish recovery-time uncertainty from strategy-ranking stability. Restoration time scaling, IDW threshold, and source-gate threshold preserve the baseline strategy ranking within the tested ranges. Crew availability is less rank-stable, with Spearman rank correlation ranging from 0.60 to 1.00. This indicates that crew scarcity can alter relative strategy performance by increasing the importance of repair sequencing, yard-to-task travel, and the spatial distribution of damaged substations. Overall, restoration-related assumptions primarily control the absolute duration of recovery, whereas topology-related assumptions mainly test the robustness of service-propagation and tract-mapping boundaries.

Discussion


## P0260 / body block 266 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Contextural Comparsion


## P0261 / body block 267 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Because a probabilistic Los Angeles earthquake restoration scenario cannot be directly validated against observations, the modeled Northridge scenario is compared with historical Northridge restoration evidence as a contextual scale check. Cagnan et al. (2006), citing LADWP’s post-Northridge restoration record, reported that approximately 50%, 75%, 90%, 95%, and 100% of LADWP customers were restored within 7, 11, 19, 26, and 168 h, respectively. The present modeled Northridge scenario gives a population-weighted T80 of 14.25 h for the unconstrained baseline and 14.55–15.25 h across logistics-aware restoration strategies. Since the historical record does not directly report T80, linear interpolation between the reported 75% and 90% customer-restoration milestones gives an approximate historical value of 13.7 h. The modeled values are therefore close to the historical partial-restoration scale.


## P0262 / body block 268 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The comparison also shows that the model should not be treated as a replay of the 1994 event. The modeled Northridge T50 is shorter than the historical 50% customer-restoration milestone, while the modeled T90 and T95 are longer. This mismatch is expected because the present framework does not model distribution feeders, customer-level switching, or utility emergency operating records directly. Instead, it evaluates a controlled source-gated transmission service proxy. The main value of the comparison is therefore confirmation that the modeled partial-restoration scale is not inconsistent with the historical Northridge restoration record.


## P0263 / body block 269 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The mechanisms underlying the modeled Northridge scenario are also consistent with prior Los Angeles restoration modeling. Cagnan et al. (2006) found that Los Angeles residents may experience earthquake-induced power outages lasting up to about 10 days, that rapidity risk varies geographically, and that restoration can be constrained by repair materials and restoration-process limitations. Cheng et al. (2024) further showed that substation damage is the key driver of census-tract-level load-shed risk in Los Angeles, while Xu et al. (2007) treated post-earthquake LADWP restoration as a scheduling problem intended to reduce average customer outage time. The present study extends these prior findings by translating substation damage into time-dependent tract-level service recovery and by evaluating how source-gated topology, tract–substation dependency, utility yard travel, crew constraints, and restoration prioritization rules reshape recovery trajectories.


## P0264 / body block 270 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Future Work


## P0265 / body block 271 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The present framework is intended as a transparent, scenario-based decision-support assessment for comparing how hazard, network dependency, repair logistics, and prioritization assumptions shape tract-level recovery outcomes. Future work can extend the present framework in several directions. First, the tract-level service representation can be refined by incorporating distribution-network topology and utility customer-feeder connection data when such data become available. This would allow transmission- and substation-level service proxies to be linked more directly to feeder-level outage and restoration patterns. Second, the tract-to-substation dependency model can be further improved by integrating utility service-territory information, feeder maps, or empirical outage records, which would reduce reliance on spatial proximity and topology-informed dependency assumptions.


## P0266 / body block 272 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The restoration component can also be expanded as more operational data become available. Future studies could calibrate damage-conditioned restoration durations using utility repair records, field inspection logs, or post-event restoration data. Road-network travel modeling could also be enhanced by incorporating post-earthquake bridge damage, road closures, congestion, emergency access restrictions, and time-varying travel conditions. In addition, the  restoration priority rules used here can be extended toward more realistic operational decision models that account for utility-specific crew dispatch protocols, material availability, mutual-aid coordination, and real-time damage information.


## P0267 / body block 273 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Further extensions can also strengthen the community-outcome interpretation. The recovery-vulnerability typology developed in this study provides a descriptive classification of recurring recovery profiles, and future work could examine the causal mechanisms linking infrastructure dependency, social vulnerability, and delayed recovery. Hospital-service restoration can also be refined by incorporating facility-level backup power, water supply, staffing, patient capacity, and road accessibility, allowing future assessments to move from infrastructure-service access toward a more comprehensive representation of hospital operational functionality.


## P0268 / body block 274 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Conclusion


## P0269 / body block 275 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

This study presents an integrated tract-level assessment framework for post-earthquake electric-power service recovery in the Los Angeles region. By linking seismic damage to transmission/substation assets with source-gated network connectivity, tract–substation service dependency, utility-yard and crew constraints, restoration-strategy comparison, and recovery-vulnerability typologies, the framework connects physical infrastructure disruption with community-level recovery outcomes. The analysis therefore moves beyond aggregate grid functionality and evaluates how restoration decisions can produce uneven service recovery across census tracts, including areas where delayed modeled restoration overlaps with higher social vulnerability and disaster-risk exposure.


## P0270 / body block 276 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The results show that post-earthquake electric-power service recovery in the Los Angeles region is shaped by the interaction of network topology, tract-level dependency, repair logistics, and social vulnerability. The source-gated topology concentrates network vulnerability in a limited set of high-impact substations, while the unconstrained baseline shows that tract-level recovery already varies spatially before crew-dispatch constraints are introduced. Under logistics-aware restoration, utility yard locations, crew availability, travel time, and prioritization  further differentiate recovery outcomes across strategies.


## P0271 / body block 277 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The strategy comparison suggests that restoration priorities should be interpreted in relation to the planning objective. Network-oriented centrality measures are useful for diagnosing structurally important substations, but network-based priority rules do not necessarily produce the fastest population- or vulnerability-weighted service recovery. Service-oriented strategies, especially those based on population impact and hospital-related priority, provide stronger non-GA alternatives for restoring service to affected communities. GA-based strategies further show that milestone recovery and cumulative recovery can favor different schedules. The fastest T80 strategy is not necessarily the strategy with the highest AUC. This supports evaluating restoration performance using multiple metrics.


## P0272 / body block 278 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The recovery-vulnerability typology provides a complementary community-level interpretation. The clustering results show that delayed restoration and social vulnerability are not perfectly aligned. Some socially vulnerable tracts recover relatively quickly in the modeled service network, while some slower-recovery tracts are not among the most socially vulnerable. Therefore, the most policy-relevant recovery-equity concern is concentrated in tracts where delayed modeled restoration overlaps with higher social vulnerability, older housing stock, and greater disaster-risk exposure. The top recovery-vulnerability hotspots provide a more targeted screening output than either recovery time or social vulnerability alone.


## P0273 / body block 279 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Future work can extend the framework by incorporating distribution-network topology, utility customer-feeder connection data, and observed outage or repair records when such data become available. The logistics component can also be refined by modeling post-earthquake road disruption, material availability, and mutual-aid coordination. A more targeted analysis could couple the tract-level service proxy with facility-level power-flow simulation to improve the representation of operational service restoration for hospitals and other critical facilities.
