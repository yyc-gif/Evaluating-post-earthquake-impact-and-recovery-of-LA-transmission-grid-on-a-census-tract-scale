## P0001 / body block 1 | Front matter

From Substation Damage to Community Recovery: Tract-Level Assessment of Post-Earthquake Power-Service Restoration in Los Angeles


## P0003 / body block 3 | Front matter

Yinchen Yi, Yutong Li, Marta C. González


## P0004 / body block 4 | Front matter

Abstract


## P0005 / body block 5 | Front matter

Major earthquakes can disrupt electric-power systems in multifaceted aspects. In metropolitan regions, post-earthquake power recovery depends not only on which transmission and substation assets are damaged, but also on how restoration resources are dispatched and which communities remain without service. This study presents a census tract-level framework for evaluating post-earthquake electric-power service disruption and recovery in the contiguous urban area of Los Angeles County. The framework reconstructs a source-gated transmission topology, simulates scenario-based substation damage using fragility functions, translates substation functionality into census-tract service availability through tract-substation dependency weights, and compares unconstrained recovery with logistics-aware restoration strategies including rule-based priorities, and Genetic Algorithm (GA)-based scheduling recommendations. Using the 2%-in-50-year seismic scenario as the primary case, the results show that network vulnerability is concentrated in a limited set of high-impact substations and that tract-level recovery is spatially uneven even before crew-dispatch constraints are introduced. Under logistics-aware restoration, strategy performance cannot be explained by network centrality alone because population exposure, tract-substation dependency, and repair sequencing jointly shape service recovery. The hospital-prioritized rule provides the fastest recovery among the tested logistics-aware strategies, while the population-impact rule provides a competitive non-optimization alternative and GA-HospitalFirst performs best among the GA-based schedules. The recovery-vulnerability typology further shows that delayed modeled restoration and social vulnerability are not perfectly aligned. A composite hotspot analysis identifies the tracts where slow recovery, social vulnerability, older housing stock, and disaster-risk exposure coincide. The proposed framework supports socio-technical risk-informed restoration planning by comparing recovery priorities and identifying communities where post-earthquake power-service delays may compound vulnerabilities and exacerbate inequities.

## P0010 / body block 11 | Front matter

Introduction


## P0011 / body block 12 | Front matter

Electric systems serve as the backbone for the normal operation of society. As key components, the functionality of substations and transmission lines is critical for the overall supply and service of electricity. When earthquakes damage the electric-power system, neighborhoods can experience different levels and durations of service disruption because local electricity access depends on which substations remain functional, how transmission connectivity is affected, and how quickly damaged assets can be restored. Post-earthquake power recovery is therefore not only a system-performance problem, but also a community-service problem, as electricity supports emergency response, healthcare operation and communication in affected area. Recent resilience research has emphasized that infrastructure recovery should not be evaluated only through aggregate system functionality, because service losses and restoration delays are often spatially uneven and socially differentiated. This perspective is particularly relevant for Los Angeles County, where seismic hazard, power service availability, population density, and socioeconomic vulnerability are unevenly distributed and compounded across space.


## P0012 / body block 13 | 1.1 Equity-aware infrastructure restoration

1.1 Equity-aware infrastructure restoration


## P0013 / body block 14 | 1.1 Equity-aware infrastructure restoration

Recent infrastructure resilience studies have increasingly shifted from system-centered performance metrics toward community-centered recovery outcomes. Logan and Guikema (2020) reframed community resilience around equitable access to essential services, emphasizing that recovery should be evaluated not only by infrastructure functionality, but also by residents’ spatially uneven access to services such as food, health care, education, water, power, sanitation, and communications. Dhakal and Zhang (2022) incorporated social equity and vulnerability into infrastructure resilience assessment by accounting for unequal distributions of functional loss and recovery time, thereby linking resilience metrics to both inequality and impacts on socially vulnerable communities. Ding et al. (2026) extended this line of work to the census-tract scale by examining the spatial-temporal dynamics of public infrastructure service inequality during hurricanes using 311 service-request data, showing that reported service requests and resolution time vary systematically across geographic exposure, social vulnerability, and disaster-risk groups. In the power-outage context, Loni and Asadi (2024) developed a data-driven index of social vulnerability to prolonged outages in California, while Lin et al. (2022) framed equity as a missing dimension in power-grid resilience and argued that recognitional, procedural, and distributional equity should be embedded into power-system planning and disaster management. Esmalian et al. (2022) further showed, through a hazard-household-infrastructure multi-agent model, that infrastructure service disruptions can impose uneven household-level hardship, highlighting the need to evaluate recovery outcomes across socially differentiated populations.


## P0014 / body block 15 | 1.1 Equity-aware infrastructure restoration

Beyond assessment, a related body of work has incorporated community characteristics into restoration planning and resource-allocation models. Chen et al. (2025a) advanced community-centered infrastructure restoration by combining population size, social vulnerability, and public facilities, such as hospitals and emergency shelters, into restoration-priority weights for interdependent power and water infrastructure recovery. Li et al. (2023) proposed a data-driven and equity-centered framework for highway restoration following flood inundation, linking damage identification, restoration scheduling, and progress monitoring while considering social vulnerability in restoration prioritization. Chen et al. (2025b) developed a human-centered infrastructure restoration framework that estimates post-disaster community demand using Bayesian updating and links demand uncertainty to restoration resource allocation for interdependent infrastructure systems. In power-grid resilience planning, Toplu-Tutay et al. (2024) incorporated equity metrics into a stochastic substation-hardening and power-flow optimization framework, approximating community-level outage impacts through census-tract-level vulnerability and well-being-loss measures.


## P0015 / body block 16 | 1.1 Equity-aware infrastructure restoration

Together, these studies show that a growing body of disaster recovery research has moved beyond purely technical system restoration and has begun to account for essential-service access and equity-aware resource allocation. This literature provides the conceptual and methodological basis for evaluating restoration outcomes by asking not only how much infrastructure functionality is restored, but also which communities regain service, when they regain it, and how recovery benefits and burdens are distributed across space.


## P0016 / body block 17 | 1.2 Substation Fragility, Repair Scheduling, Lifeline Restoration, and Critical Service Restoration

1.2 Substation Fragility, Repair Scheduling, Lifeline Restoration, and Critical Service Restoration


## P0017 / body block 18 | 1.2 Substation Fragility, Repair Scheduling, Lifeline Restoration, and Critical Service Restoration

A parallel stream of research has developed repair-scheduling, routing, and restoration-optimization models for disrupted critical infrastructure systems. Xu et al. (2026) reviewed post-disaster repair-resource scheduling models and showed that past research has matured around restoration objectives, scheduling and routing decisions, operation constraints, uncertainty treatment, and solution algorithms. Within this stream, Li et al. (2019) developed a joint optimization model that simultaneously assigns restoration tasks to multiple teams, schedules component repairs, and routes crews among geographically distributed damaged components while evaluating recovery performance using maximal network flow and unmet demand. Loggins et al. (2019) developed the CRISIS decision-support model to schedule repairs of damaged civil infrastructure according to stakeholder-defined priorities for dependent social infrastructure services, capturing interdependencies between civil systems and critical social functions. Related work has also expanded restoration scheduling beyond purely component-based repair decisions. Shahverdi et al. (2025) formulated a decision-support model that coordinates specialized repair crews across interdependent power, water, and transportation lifelines to prioritize critical societal services such as hospitals. Shahverdi et al. (2024) further modeled personnel as “human infrastructure,” emphasizing that critical services require not only functional buildings and lifeline support but also skilled personnel who can remain in the affected area and travel to their workplaces. Recent power-restoration models have also addressed uncertainty-aware and adaptive decision-making: Jiang et al. (2025) proposed a predict-then-optimize framework that combines repair-duration uncertainty prediction with equity-constrained power restoration sequencing, and Li et al. (2026) developed a post-earthquake electricity-gas restoration framework that jointly models damage-information collection and repair-vehicle routing under partial observability.


## P0018 / body block 19 | 1.2 Substation Fragility, Repair Scheduling, Lifeline Restoration, and Critical Service Restoration

For earthquake-specific power-system recovery, prior studies have coupled seismic damage, network functionality, and restoration decisions at the system or component level. Xie et al. (2024) developed a resilience-based optimisation framework for post-earthquake power-system restoration by linking seismic-intensity attenuation, fragility-based component operational-state assessment, graph-based system functionality metrics, and recovery-path optimisation. Fu et al. (2024) advanced seismic vulnerability assessment of electrical substation systems by combining hybrid equipment fragility functions, Bayesian updating with seismic damage-survey data, and a Bayesian-network representation of substation topology and redundancy to infer system functionality and component importance. These studies provide foundations for modeling how earthquake-induced component damage affects power-system functionality and recovery priorities.


## P0019 / body block 20 | 1.2 Substation Fragility, Repair Scheduling, Lifeline Restoration, and Critical Service Restoration

Several studies are especially relevant to earthquake-induced electric-power risk and restoration within Los Angeles County. Monte Carlo-based studies have linked earthquake ground motion, component fragility, and electric transmission-network performance through power-flow analysis. Shinozuka et al. (2007) evaluated seismic disruption of the Los Angeles City power transmission network by sampling component damage under multiple earthquake scenarios and assessing resulting network performance, while Johnson et al. (2020) similarly used Monte Carlo simulation and power-flow analysis to estimate demand not served under seismic damage. Other scenario-based work has emphasized the representation of regional earthquake hazard and downstream outage consequences. Soleimani et al. (2021) developed a Los Angeles City multi-hazard earthquake scenario selection method that preserves risk estimates for ground shaking, liquefaction, and surface fault rupture while reducing computational burden. Building on these scenarios, Cheng et al. (2024) quantified census-tract-level electric-power risk in Los Angeles City by combining probabilistic earthquake and damage scenarios, HAZUS-based component damage, DC load-flow analysis, and tract-level load-shed allocation, showing that substation damage is a major driver of outage risk and that solar-plus-storage benefits may remain spatially and socially uneven. Other studies have modeled post-earthquake electric-power restoration processes in Los Angeles more directly. Çağnan et al. (2006) provided an early City of Los Angeles-specific benchmark for post-earthquake electric-power restoration by using discrete-event simulation to translate hazard-driven substation damage into restoration rapidity risk. Their results showed that outage duration can vary geographically across Los Angeles Department of Water and Power (LADWP) service areas and can be shaped as much by repair-team and material bottlenecks as by initial component damage. Xu et al. (2007) further formulated a stochastic optimization model for scheduling LADWP post-earthquake inspection, damage assessment, and repair tasks, using restoration curves and outage-duration metrics to compare optimized schedules with baseline or utility-informed restoration schedules. More broadly, logistics-oriented restoration studies such as Cavdaroglu et al. (2013) show that repair sequencing, crew assignment, travel time, precedence relationships, and repair uncertainty can substantially affect infrastructure recovery trajectories.


## P0020 / body block 21 | 1.2 Substation Fragility, Repair Scheduling, Lifeline Restoration, and Critical Service Restoration

Taken together, this body of work provides the engineering and logistical foundation for modeling earthquake-induced power-system disruption and recovery. These studies have advanced power-flow-based outage assessment, substation fragility modeling, repair scheduling and logistics-aware recovery analysis. Their contributions show how damaged infrastructure components, network constraints, resource limitations, and restoration operations shape power-system recovery trajectories.

## P0022 / body block 23 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Despite these advances, a specific gap remains for earthquake-induced urban power-service recovery: few studies perform a single metropolitan earthquake power-service recovery assessment that links physical damage, restoration operations, and tract-level recovery disparities. This gap limits the ability to evaluate not only how quickly the power system recovers, but also how physical damage, restoration logistics, and prioritization rules translate into uneven power-service recovery across census tracts and vulnerable communities.


## P0023 / body block 24 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Building on prior studies of earthquake-driven power-system risk, fragility-based component damage, and resource-constrained restoration scheduling, this study asks three questions:


## P0024 / body block 25 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

How do earthquake-induced substation damage and source-gated transmission network connectivity translate into census-tract-level power-service disruption and recovery trajectories?


## P0025 / body block 26 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

How do utility yard logistics, crew constraints, and restoration prioritization strategies affect population-weighted recovery and critical-service restoration?


## P0026 / body block 27 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Which census tracts experience prolonged or vulnerability-amplified recovery, and how can these outcomes be characterized through tract-level recovery-vulnerability typologies?


## P0027 / body block 28 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

To address these questions, this study develops a tract-level assessment of post-earthquake electric-power service disruption and recovery for the study area. The assessment couples scenario-specific seismic intensity, substation fragility, source-gated transmission/substation topology, tract-to-substation dependency mapping, damage-conditioned stochastic repair durations, road-network travel from repair-yard origins, alternative restoration-prioritization strategies, and consolidated metric-based tract typology identification. This structure translates earthquake-induced substation damage into power-service recovery trajectories for individual census tracts. Recovery outcomes are evaluated using tract-level service metrics, including population-weighted recovery time and vulnerability-related recovery typologies derived from social, demographic, built-environment, and simulated recovery indicators. A sensitivity analysis further examines how assumptions on crew availability, repair-time scale, Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) dependency threshold, and source-gate threshold affect absolute recovery times and strategy-ranking stability. This study combines established hazard, fragility, network-dependency, repair-logistics, and vulnerability-assessment components into a transparent workflow for evaluating earthquake-specific power-service recovery and community-level recovery disparities.


## P0028 / body block 29 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Methodology


## P0030 / body block 31 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Figure 1: Study Workflow. The framework consists of five core modules: (1) Topology and Dependency Construction, which builds the source-gated transmission network and tract-substation dependency mapping; (2) Seismic Damage Simulation, which estimates substation damage states using fragility functions; (3) Damage-to-Service Translation, which translates substation-level physical damage into tract-level service availability; (4) Recovery Modeling, which simulates restoration trajectories under unconstrained baselines and logistics-aware scheduling; and (5) Outputs and Interpretation, which evaluates population-weighted recovery metrics and identifies recovery-vulnerability hotspots.


## P0031 / body block 32 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Figure 1 summarizes the workflow used to evaluate post-earthquake electric-power service disruption and recovery in the study area. The workflow begins with five groups of input data: transmission lines and substations, scenario Peak Ground Acceleration (PGA) fields, roads and utility yards, hospitals, and tract-level attributes. These inputs are then processed through five analytical modules: topology and dependency construction, seismic damage simulation, damage-to-service translation, recovery modeling, and outputs interpretation. Together, the workflow translates scenario-specific substation damage into tract-level service recovery trajectories and recovery-vulnerability outcomes.


## P0032 / body block 33 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

First, a transmission topology is constructed by combining substation point records with high-voltage transmission-line geometries. The resulting graph is used to represent network connectivity, identify source-connected components, and compute structural criticality measures such as degree, betweenness, and connectivity-related impact on the largest connected component. In parallel, a tract-to-substation dependency matrix is developed to allocate substation functionality to census tracts using topology-informed spatial proximity.


## P0033 / body block 34 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Second, seismic hazard intensity is assigned to each substation from scenario PGA fields. The analysis considers three historical earthquake scenarios, the 1933 Long Beach, 1971 San Fernando, and 1994 Northridge events, together with a probabilistic 2%-in-50-year scenario. Lognormal fragility functions are then used in a Monte Carlo procedure to sample discrete substation damage states. These damage states are mapped to residual functionality levels and damage-conditioned stochastic restoration durations.


## P0034 / body block 35 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Third, substation damage is translated into tract-level service disruption through a damage-to-service propagation module. Residual substation functionality is routed through the source-gated transmission topology and combined with tract-to-substation dependency weights to estimate tract-level power-service availability.


## P0035 / body block 36 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Fourth, recovery modeling is conducted under both an unconstrained baseline and a logistics-aware restoration setting. The unconstrained baseline characterizes tract-level recovery trajectories based on damage-state restoration durations without crew-dispatch constraints. The logistics-aware restoration model incorporates utility yard origins, road-network travel times, and crew constraints. Within this model, recovery outcomes are compared across rule-based prioritization strategies and Genetic Algorithm (GA)-based optimization benchmarks to evaluate how resource-constrained task sequencing affects tract-level service restoration.


## P0036 / body block 37 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

Finally, recovery outcomes are interpreted using weighted recovery metrics, sensitivity analysis, and recovery-vulnerability typologies. Population- and vulnerability-weighted recovery trajectories are used to compare restoration strategies, while sensitivity analysis evaluates how major modeling assumptions affect recovery time and strategy-ranking stability. Simulated recovery indicators are then combined with social, demographic, built-environment, and hazard-related tract attributes. These features are standardized and fed into K-means clustering to identify census tracts where delayed recovery and vulnerability-related concerns coincide.


## P0037 / body block 38 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

The pipeline integrates heterogeneous geospatial datasets. The physical power grid is represented using substation inventories and high-voltage transmission-line geometries obtained from the California Energy Commission (n.d.) and Homeland Infrastructure Foundation-Level Data (n.d.). Scenario-specific ground-motion fields are obtained from U.S. Geological Survey ShakeMap products (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.). Census tract boundaries are obtained from U.S. Census Bureau TIGER/Line data (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b), with demographic attributes joined from the American Community Survey (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-a). Social Vulnerability Index attributes are obtained from the CDC/ATSDR Social Vulnerability Index database (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention/Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry/Geospatial Research, Analysis, and Services Program, 2022), National Risk Index attributes from the FEMA National Risk Index dataset (Federal Emergency Management Agency, n.d.), and hospital locations from California Health and Human Services open data (California Health and Human Services Agency, n.d.).


## P0038 / body block 39 | 1.3 Focus of This Study

The study area covers the contiguous urbanized portion of Los Angeles County south of the San Gabriel Mountains, including the Los Angeles Basin, the San Fernando Valley, the San Gabriel Valley, and the Long Beach-Harbor area. This region represents the county’s primary continuous urban corridor, where dense population, critical facilities, and major electric-power infrastructure are concentrated across connected basin, valley, and coastal-plain settings.

## P0043 / body block 45 | 2.1.1 Topological Reconstruction of the Transmission Network

2.1.1 Topological Reconstruction of the Transmission Network


## P0044 / body block 46 | 2.1.1 Topological Reconstruction of the Transmission Network

To transforming raw power grid geometries into a network graph, transmission line records are first decomposed into individual line-string components so that their vertices and segments could be processed consistently. Then, to reconcile spatial offsets between the line and substation layers, the endpoints of each line-string component are snapped to nearby substations. Endpoints within 150 m of a substation are directly assigned to the nearest substation, whereas endpoints within 150 to 300 m are assigned only when the nearest candidate is clearly distinguished from the second-nearest candidate, based on a minimum distance margin of 75 m and a nearest-to-second-nearest distance ratio not exceeding 0.75. To preserve existing line-line connectivity, when a junction vertex cluster satisfies the snapping criteria, all vertices in the cluster are moved together to the corresponding nearby substation. Nearby unresolved line endpoints were merged within a 75 m tolerance as a conservative geometry-repair step.


## P0045 / body block 47 | 2.1.1 Topological Reconstruction of the Transmission Network

Substations located near the interior of a line-string component are incorporated by splitting the line at the substation’s orthogonal projection point when the substation-to-line distance is within 10 m. These splits are subject to compatibility checks based on line ownership and voltage family, and a short connector representing the actual substation-to-line projection line is added when necessary. The processed geometries are then converted into an undirected weighted graph Gphys=(Vphys,ℰphys), by treating line-string vertices as graph nodes and consecutive vertex pairs as legnth-weighted edges. For efficient distance calculations, each substation is registered to the nearest node in Gphys.


## P0046 / body block 48 | 2.1.1 Topological Reconstruction of the Transmission Network

Unlike the above mentioned physical weighted graph Gphys, the topological network G=(V,ℰ) is constructed by straight connections between each pair of directly connected substations. A pair is considered directly adjacent only if the vertices listed in the shortest path in Gphys are not associated with any other substation. Undirected weighted edges are constructed with the weight being the Dijkstra calculated along-grid shortest path length in Gphys. This reduced graph G preserves the connectivity relationships required for centrality, fragmentation, source-accessibility, and restoration analyses, but it is not interpreted as an electrical power-flow equivalent of the original transmission system.

Post-earthquake restoration is modeled as a resource-constrained multi-depot routing and scheduling problem. The formulation enforces several operational constraints.


## P0191 / body block 193 | 2.5.1 Resource-Constrained Dispatch Model

The system state is defined by the set of remaining damaged substations and the crew states. After the repair is completed, the task is marked complete, the crew’s location and next availability time are updated, and the substation is treated as restored. 2.5.2 Rule-based Restoration Sequencing


## P0206 / body block 208 | 2.5.2 Rule-based Restoration Sequencing

Rule-based sequencing logic, which is primarily based on the variations in the weights of substation metrics. We evaluate seven strategies:


## P0207 / body block 209 | 2.5.2 Rule-based Restoration Sequencing

(1) Impact-first. Prioritizes structurally important substations to accelerate restoration of network connectivity. Substations are ranked by impact centrality first, betweenness centrality second, and node degree third.


## P0208 / body block 210 | 2.5.2 Rule-based Restoration Sequencing

(2) Betweenness-first. Prioritizes substations that serve as bridging nodes in the transmission graph. Substations are ranked by betweenness centrality first and node degree second.


## P0209 / body block 211 | 2.5.2 Rule-based Restoration Sequencing

(3) Degree-first. Prioritizes substation connected to large number of other substations. Substations are ranked by node degree first and betweenness centrality second.


## P0210 / body block 212 | 2.5.2 Rule-based Restoration Sequencing

(4) Closeness-first. Prioritizes substations with short paths of connections to the rest of the network. Substations are ranked by closeness centrality first and node degree second.


## P0211 / body block 213 | 2.5.2 Rule-based Restoration Sequencing

(5) Impact-first (Population). Prioritizes substations expected to restore service to the largest number of people. Substations are ranked by population impact, computed as a node-level population-importance proxy from tract dependency weights and tract population.


## P0212 / body block 214 | 2.5.2 Rule-based Restoration Sequencing

(6) Hospital-first. Prioritizes substations serving tracts that contain any hospital. Substations are ranked first by the number of mapped hospital-containing census tract links and second by total mapped population.


## P0213 / body block 215 | 2.5.2 Rule-based Restoration Sequencing

(7) Random. Generates a fixed-seed random order of damaged substations and serves as a reproducible baseline representing uncoordinated recovery.


## P0214 / body block 216 | 2.5.2 Rule-based Restoration Sequencing

Importantly, these policies differ only in task selection. All policies are evaluated under the same operational constraints, allowing a clear comparison of decision logic.

## P0264 / body block 267 | 2.6 Tract-Level Typology with K-Means

The clustering is performed using the K-means algorithm, which partitions observations into K clusters based on minimizing Within Cluster Sum of Square(WCSS) variance (MacQueen, 1967; Lloyd, 1982). Candidate solutions are evaluated for K=1,…,10. Let WCSSK denote the inertia obtained for K clusters. The selected number of clusters, K∗, is determined using using the elbow criterion (Thorndike, 1953), operationalized as the candidate point with the maximum perpendicular distance to the line segment connecting the first and last points of the WCSS curve.. The variables summarized in Table 3 are used for clustering analysis. These variables encompass features representing power supply robustness, built environment characteristics, socio-demographic factors, and natural hazard risk exposure. To limit the disproportionate influence of strongly correlated features, one representative variable is retained from each group of correlated variables. The clustering analysis is used to identify distinct census-tract recovery-vulnerability typologies and to determine which groups exhibit the combined characteristics of delayed restoration, elevated social vulnerability, and greater disaster-risk exposure.

## P0278 / body block 282 | 2.6 Tract-Level Typology with K-Means

Substation importance depends on which centrality is considered. Degree centrality captures local connectivity, but a high degree does not reliably translate into system-wide consequence. Betweenness centrality highlights nodes that concentrate shortest-path routing and therefore represent transmission bottlenecks. Impact (λ2-based) centrality captures the contribution of a substation to global network fragmentation. Taken together, these centralities imply that restoration priorities should not be guided solely by substations with large number of transmission line connections.


## P0279 / body block 283 | 2.6 Tract-Level Typology with K-Means

Notably, when the power grid fragments into disconnected sub-networks, transmission lines would no longer connect the available supply to demand across those sub-networks, so power outage can persist even if some local assets remain functional. The percolation curves in Figure 2C track the network's LCC fraction as substations are removed. Under random removal, connectivity decreases gradually, with only two more pronounced drops around the 33rd and 67th node, respectively. In contrast, targeted removal takes out nodes ranked by the orders of various centrality metrics calculated on the original graph, so each removal degrades the network structure more than the random counterpart. Within the first 35 degree-targeted removals, the LCC fraction drops to below 0.2, long before the random case reaches comparable degradation. This indicates that the LCC decomposes much faster under targeted removal, reflecting the concentrated impact on network connectivity from a small group of high-importance substations.


## P0280 / body block 284 | 2.6 Tract-Level Typology with K-Means

This result indicates that the source-gated transmission topology is not uniformly vulnerable. Network vulnerability is concentrated in a small group of substations whose disruption can fragment the transmission network more rapidly than random outages. This topological concentration provides the structural basis for later restoration-strategy comparisons, because repairing highly connected or high-impact substations can influence not only local service restoration but also the source connectivity of network components.

The key contrast is not that topology-only rules are suboptimal, but that different recovery metrics emphasize different service beneficiaries and different portions of the recovery trajectory. The topology-oriented rules restore nodes that are structurally important in the graph, but those nodes are not necessarily the substations with the strongest tract-level population or SVI-weighted service consequences. In the 2%-in-50-year scenario, the hospital-first and impact-population-first rules reach T80 substantially earlier than the degree-, betweenness-, closeness-, and impact-𝜆2-first rules. This reveals that population exposure, vulnerable-population exposure, and tract-substation dependency determine a repair sequence's efficacy far better than network topology alone.

Notably, the population- and SVI-weighted metrics are similar but not identical. Across most strategies, SVI-weighted T80 is slightly earlier than or approximately equal to population-weighted T80, indicating that the restoration sequence happens to recover many SVI-weighted service dependencies at least as quickly as the population-weighted average. However, the magnitude of the population-SVI gap varies by strategy. This difference is the key equity-relevant finding: restoration strategies do not only differ in aggregate speed, but also in how their repair order aligns with the spatial distributions of population exposure and social vulnerability. As expected, the unconstrained case yields the largest AUC because it represents an upper-bound reference in which all substation recovery processes begin at t=0. It does not include crew-capacity, task-sequencing, depot-travel, or inter-task travel constraints. Logistics-aware schedules introduce nonnegative repair-start delays and consequently reduce the area under the service-availability curve. The unconstrained case is therefore used as a theoretical reference rather than a competing restoration strategy.

## P0309 / body block 314 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The sensitivity analysis isolates the impact of key modeling assumptions on the baseline results. The tested ranges are grouped into two categories, namely restoration-related assumptions and topology-related assumptions. The restoration-related assumptions include crew availability and restoration time scaling, which directly affect repair capacity and damage-conditioned restoration duration. The topology-related assumptions include the IDW dependency threshold and source-gate threshold, which affect tract-substation dependency mapping and source-gated network accessibility.


## P0310 / body block 315 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Figure 7 and Table 5 summarize the sensitivity of population-weighted recovery time T80pop to restoration-related and topology-related assumptions under the 2%-in-50-year scenario. The results show that population-weighted recovery milestones are primarily controlled by restoration-related assumptions. Crew availability produces the largest within-strategy change in T80pop, with a maximum shift of 49.50 h across the tested range. This reflects the substantial effect of crew capacity on parallel restoration execution. Restoration-time scaling produces a comparable maximum shift of 45.10 h, because it directly stretches or compresses the duration of damage-conditioned restoration tasks across the system. By contrast, topology-related parameters have smaller effects on aggregate T80pop. Varying the IDW dependency threshold changes how tract demand is distributed across substations, but its maximum effect on aggregate T80pop is 2.20 h. This suggests that the population-weighted recovery milestone is relatively insensitive to moderate changes in weak tract-substation links. The source-gate threshold has a larger but still subtle effect, with a maximum T80pop change of 6.00 h. This threshold affects which partially functional substations are allowed to participate in source-connected service propagation, but within the tested range it does not substantially alter the leading group of restoration strategies.


## P0311 / body block 316 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The sensitivity results also distinguish recovery-time uncertainty from strategy-ranking stability. Restoration-time scaling, IDW threshold, and source-gate threshold largely preserve the baseline strategy ranking within the tested ranges, with Spearman rank correlations of 0.99-1.00, 0.98-1.00, and 0.95-1.00, respectively. Crew availability is less rank-stable, with Spearman rank correlation ranging from 0.23 to 1.00. This indicates that crew scarcity and crew abundance can change relative strategy performance by altering the importance of repair sequencing, yard-to-task travel, and the spatial distribution of damaged substations. Overall, restoration-related assumptions primarily control the absolute duration of recovery, whereas topology-related assumptions mainly test the robustness of service-propagation and tract-mapping boundaries.

Discussion


## P0316 / body block 322 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Contextual Comparsion


## P0317 / body block 323 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Because a probabilistic Los Angeles earthquake restoration scenario cannot be directly validated against observations, the modeled Northridge scenario is compared with historical Northridge restoration evidence as a contextual scale check. Çağnan et al. (2006), citing LADWP’s post-Northridge restoration record, reported that approximately 50%, 75%, 90%, 95%, and 100% of LADWP utility customers were restored within 7, 11, 19, 26, and 168 h, respectively. The present modeled Northridge scenario gives a population-weighted T80 of 14.25 h for the unconstrained baseline and 14.55-15.25 h across logistics-aware restoration strategies. Since the historical record does not directly report T80, linear interpolation between the reported 75% and 90% customer-restoration milestones gives an approximate historical value of 13.7 h. The modeled values are therefore close to the historical partial-restoration scale.


## P0318 / body block 324 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The comparison also shows that the model should not be treated as a replay of the 1994 event. The modeled Northridge T50 is shorter than the historical 50% customer-restoration milestone, while the modeled T90 and T95 are longer. This mismatch is expected because the present framework does not model distribution feeders, customer-level switching, or utility emergency operating records directly. Instead, it evaluates a controlled source-gated transmission service proxy. The main value of the comparison is therefore confirmation that the modeled partial-restoration scale is not inconsistent with the historical Northridge restoration record.


## P0319 / body block 325 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The mechanisms underlying the modeled Northridge scenario are also consistent with prior Los Angeles restoration modeling. Çağnan et al. (2006) found that Los Angeles residents may experience earthquake-induced power outages lasting up to about 10 days, that rapidity risk varies geographically, and that restoration can be constrained by repair materials and restoration-process limitations. Cheng et al. (2024) further showed that substation damage is the key driver of census-tract-level load-shed risk in Los Angeles, while Xu et al. (2007) treated post-earthquake LADWP restoration as a scheduling problem intended to reduce average customer outage time. The present study extends these prior findings by translating substation damage into time-dependent tract-level service recovery and by evaluating how source-gated topology, tract-substation dependency, utility yard travel, crew constraints, and restoration prioritization rules reshape recovery outcomes. The tested prioritization rules show a trade-off between optimizing overall network connectivity and minimizing the immediate impact of power outages on the population. While the network centrality-based rules allow for prompt reconstruction of the physical grid, the rules informed by tract characteristics perform better in shortening the outage duration for highly populated and vulnerable tracts.


## P0320 / body block 326 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Future Work


## P0321 / body block 327 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The present framework is intended as a transparent, scenario-based decision-support assessment for comparing how hazard, network dependency, repair logistics, and prioritization assumptions shape tract-level recovery outcomes. Future work can extend the present framework in several directions. First, the tract-level service representation can be refined by incorporating distribution-network topology and utility customer-feeder connection data when such data become available. This would allow transmission- and substation-level service proxies to be linked more directly to feeder-level outage and restoration patterns. Second, the tract-to-substation dependency model can be further improved by integrating utility service-territory information, feeder maps, or empirical outage records, which would reduce reliance on spatial proximity and topology-informed dependency assumptions.


## P0322 / body block 328 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The restoration component can also be expanded as more operational data become available. Future studies could calibrate damage-conditioned restoration durations using utility repair records, field inspection logs, or post-event restoration data. Road-network travel modeling could also be enhanced by incorporating post-earthquake bridge damage, road closures, congestion, emergency access restrictions, and time-varying travel conditions. In addition, the  restoration priority rules used here can be extended toward more realistic operational decision models that account for utility-specific crew dispatch protocols, material availability, mutual-aid coordination, and real-time damage information.


## P0323 / body block 329 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Further extensions can also strengthen the community-outcome interpretation. The recovery-vulnerability typology developed in this study provides a descriptive classification of recurring recovery profiles, and future work could examine the causal mechanisms linking infrastructure dependency, social vulnerability, and delayed recovery. Hospital-service restoration can also be refined by incorporating facility-level backup power, water supply, staffing, patient capacity, and road accessibility, allowing future assessments to move from infrastructure-service access toward a more comprehensive representation of hospital operational functionality.


## P0324 / body block 330 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Conclusion


## P0325 / body block 331 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

This study presents an integrated tract-level assessment framework for post-earthquake electric-power service recovery in the contiguous urban areas of Los Angeles County. By linking seismic damage to substation assets, source-gated transmission network connectivity, tract-substation service dependency, utility-yard and crew constraints, restoration-strategy comparison, and recovery-vulnerability typologies, the framework connects physical infrastructure disruption with community-level recovery outcomes. The analysis evaluates how restoration decisions shape recovery trajectories and, under one representative strategy, assesses how uneven recovery service availability and socio-economic characteristics interact across census tracts, identifying areas where delayed restoration overlaps with higher social vulnerability and disaster-risk exposure.


## P0326 / body block 332 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The results show that post-earthquake electric-power service recovery in the study area is shaped by the interaction of network topology, tract-level dependency, repair logistics, and social vulnerability. The source-gated topology concentrates network vulnerability in a limited set of high-impact substations, while the unconstrained baseline shows that tract-level recovery already varies spatially before crew-dispatch constraints are introduced. Under logistics-aware restoration, utility yard locations, crew availability, travel time, and prioritization rules further differentiate recovery outcomes.


## P0327 / body block 333 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The strategy comparison indicates that restoration priorities should be selected according to the planning objective. Network centrality measures are useful for identifying substations that are structurally important to transmission-network connectivity, but service-oriented strategies, especially those based on population impact and hospital-related priority, minimize the durations of service outages. GA-based strategies further show that milestone recovery, cumulative recovery, and complete recovery can favor different restoration schedules and should therefore servce as complementary performance metrics. Under the 2%-in-50-year scenario, the hospital-first rule achieves the earliest population- and SVI-weighted T80 and the highest corresponding AUC values among the logistics-aware strategies.


## P0328 / body block 334 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

The recovery-vulnerability typology provides an additional community-level interpretation. The clustering results show that delayed restoration and social vulnerability are not perfectly aligned. Some socially vulnerable tracts recover relatively quickly in the modeled service network, while some slower-recovery tracts are not among the most socially vulnerable. Therefore, the most policy-relevant recovery-equity concern is concentrated in tracts where delayed restoration overlaps with higher social vulnerability, older housing stock, and greater disaster-risk exposure. The top recovery-vulnerability hotspots provide a more targeted screening output than either recovery time or social vulnerability alone.


## P0329 / body block 335 | 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis

These findings address the study’s three core research questions. First, earthquake-induced substation damage translates to tract-level disruption via the combined effects of spatially varying ground motion, source-gated transmission network connectivity, and tract-substation dependency, causing spatially uneven outages and differentiated layers of service importance before repair logistics are considered. Second, utility-yard logistics, crew constraints, travel times, and restoration priorities materially affect recovery. In the primary scenario, service-oriented prioritization strategies, especially hospital-oriented choices, outperform network-centrality rules in restoring power services to populations and critical hospital facilities. Finally, clustering typologies and hotspots reveal that prolonged, vulnerability-amplified recovery occurs in hotspot tracts around central Los Angeles County where delayed infrastructure restoration overlaps directly with existing socioeconomic and disaster-risk burdens.
