## P0006 / body block 6 | Front matter

Introduction


## P0007 / body block 7 | Front matter

The high-voltage substations and transmission lines in Southern California face serious seismic threats. When earthquakes damage the electric system, neighborhoods in the region would experience a varying range of levels and durations of impacts, as the local access to electricity depends on which substations remain functional and how quickly they can be repaired.


## P0009 / body block 9 | Front matter

Evaluating post-earthquake power restoration is important because electricity is essential for economic and social activities, especially in densely populated urban areas. Since the impacts of earthquakes are not spatially uniform, an evaluation framework should quantify the impacts through population-weighted recovery indicators, tract-level restoration dispersion, time to restore hospital service, equity in restoration waiting times, network criticality of affected assets, and estimates of the loss in power supply at the network and substation levels.


## P0011 / body block 11 | Front matter

This project identifies and analyzes such unequal levels of exposure and recovery across the census tracts in the City of Los Angeles by modeling the power grid as a network of supply and demand: substations on the supply side, census tracts on the demand side, connected by tract-specific “supply weights.” We question which substations are structurally critical for tract-level power service, how selected damage scenarios propagate through that network, and how different repair priorities influence the size of the population served by restoration.


## P0013 / body block 13 | Front matter

Prior studies have shown the value of modeling earthquake-driven power-system risk using scenario ground motion, fragility-based component damage, and resource-constrained restoration scheduling. This project builds on existing literature by adding power transmission centrality network analysis and tract typologies derived from PCA and k-means clustering to evaluate both system recovery and equity outcomes in Los Angeles. We build a topological transmission network, simulate substation-level shaking intensities based on three historical earthquakes with distinct spatial footprints, and use fragility functions with Monte Carlo sampling to estimate substation damage states and expected recovery times. We then translate substation disruption into tract-level outage probabilities and simulate restoration under unconstrained and logistics-aware conditions to compare prioritization strategies based on various centrality measures.


## P0014 / body block 14 | Front matter

Literature Review


## P0015 / body block 15 | Front matter

Among the literature on modeling earthquake risks, the recent studies by Soleimani et al. (2021) and Cheng et al. (2024) introduce novel approaches to simulate scenarios realistically. Soleimani et al. develop a multihazard method to measure earthquake risk to avoid any hazard type overrepresenting the model and results. They first map each earthquake’s ground shaking, liquefaction, and surface fault rupture to create consistent hazard maps. From a large set of possible ruptures, they screen events based on their contribution to overall risk. They then use a mixed-integer linear program to select a smaller set of representative ruptures with probabilities that reproduce the full hazard distribution. This smaller set is resampled to generate the final collection of scenarios. In a case study on the City of Los Angeles, the 351 selected scenarios produce results matching the simulations based on the full rupture set, which reduces computation time while maintaining accuracy (Soleimani et al., 2021).


## P0016 / body block 16 | Front matter

Moving forward, Cheng et al. (2024) simulate 351 earthquake scenarios generated by Soleimani et al. (2021), each with 12 possible damage outcomes, to model the seismic risk in the City of Los Angeles at the census tract level. They employ the HAZUS risk modeling tool developed by the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) with a Direct Current (DC) load flow model to track delivered power and load shedding (the intentional limiting or closing of power supply to prevent system overloading) over a nine-period restoration timeline. Cheng et al. find that substation damage causes most of the outages, generator damage causes secondary effects, and transmission line damage makes minimal impacts. The pattern concentrates the risks of load shedding in northern and central Los Angeles tracts. In addition, Cheng et al. map system-level outages to census tracts through distance-weighted allocation. Modeling the solar plus storage adoption levels of  2030, they suggest that solar plus storage reduces early-stage load shedding, but the benefits are uneven and favor higher-income, homeowner-occupied areas (Cheng et al., 2024).


## P0017 / body block 17 | Front matter

Literature that models the impacts of earthquakes on the electrical grid include the works by Shinozuka et al. (2007) and Johnson et al. (2020), both applying Monte Carlo simulation (MCS). Shinozuka et al. first calculate the Peak Ground Acceleration (PGA), a measure of earthquake intensity, at 52 receiving stations across 50 scenarios of historic earthquakes in the City of Los Angeles, such as the 1994 Northridge earthquake that made the city black out for the first time in history. Shinozuka et al. then model fragility curves, each representing the probability that a component fails given a certain PGA (Shinozuka et al., 2007). The fragility curves simulate how individual components may be damaged and generate 20 different possible outcomes of network damage for each scenario of earthquakes, reflecting a standard MCS practice of repeated trials (Radu et al., 2018). In turn, they employ power flow analysis to check how earthquakes disrupt power supply, fluctuate voltages, and disconnect transmission lines to quantify the network stability (Shinozuka et al., 2007). Similarly, Johnson et al. use fragility curves to estimate the likelihood that individual grid components fail under specific levels of seismic shaking. Their MCS then randomly samples component failure states based on each selected earthquake scenario’s fragility curve. It allows them to analyze the resulting damaged grid with a power flow solver to calculate Demand Not Served (DNS), or the loss of power supply (Johnson et al.,2020).


## P0018 / body block 18 | Front matter

Studies by Çag˘nan et. al (2006) and Xu et al. (2007) cover methodologies to model the post-earthquake restoration of power systems. Çag˘nan et. al (2006) combine a seismic hazard model, a damage model developed using fragility curves and MCS, and a discrete-event restoration simulation that tracks inspections, repairs, crew travel, and resource constraints. Running multiple MCS scenarios, Çag˘nan et. al produce restoration curves that show how quickly power is restored and identify risks of long outages, uneven recovery across zones, and shortages in crews or equipment. Çag˘nan et. al show that increasing repair crews most effectively reduces long-duration outages, and they reflect considerations for equitable restoration across geographies (Çag˘nan et. al, 2006). Similarly, Xu et al. (2007) simulate post-earthquake recovery of the Los Angeles power system by scheduling inspections, damage assessments, and repairs across substations to reduce outages. They use restoration curves to track how power recovers after disruptions. The model by Xu et al. minimizes the average outage, measured by SAIDIEQ (the area above the restoration curve). They use a genetic algorithm to schedule tasks and evaluate the resulting recovery in a simulation, which reduces total outage time compared to standard approaches (Xu et al., 2007).


## P0019 / body block 19 | Front matter

Cavdaroglu et al. (2013) introduce a model of restoration task scheduling that supports realistic modeling of repair crews’ travel time and path. Cavdaroglu et al. frame recovery as a co-optimization process of repairs, crew assignment, and scheduling, depending on abstractions of travel time, precedence, and repair uncertainty. Their model prioritizes repairs based on the amount of service restored per unit time and allocates tasks to crews to accelerate overall system restoration (Cavdaroglu et al., 2013).


## P0020 / body block 20 | Front matter

Despite the extensive literature, three methodological gaps challenge the application of existing models in real-world disaster response:


## P0021 / body block 21 | Front matter

Disconnection Between Physical Damage and Functional Restoration: Existing frameworks often rely on deterministic repair tables (e.g., HAZUS) that conflate physical repair (which takes weeks) with functional restoration (which takes hours/days via grid redundancy). This "physics-only" approach systematically overestimates outage durations compared to historical records (e.g., Northridge 1994).


## P0022 / body block 22 | Front matter

Fragmentation of Fragility and Logistics Models: Current research operates in silos: engineering studies (e.g., Cheng et al., 2024) quantify damage but ignore post-disaster resource bottlenecks, while operations research studies (e.g., Xu et al., 2007) optimize scheduling but lack hazard-consistent damage inputs. There is a lack of integrated frameworks that couple stochastic fragility modeling with resource-constrained queuing simulations.


## P0023 / body block 23 | Front matter

Absence of Equity-Centric Scheduling Evaluation: Traditional recovery metrics focus on aggregate efficiency (e.g., minimizing total system downtime), masking spatial disparities that affect vulnerable communities. This study quantifies how specific engineering prioritization strategies (e.g., prioritizing hospitals vs. population ce) contribute to inequality at the granular census tract level, as suggested in the study by Cheng et al. (2024).


## P0024 / body block 24 | Front matter

Consequently, this study proposes an integrated computational pipeline comprised of four sequential modules designed to bridge these gaps:


## P0025 / body block 25 | Front matter

Fragility Modeling: Damage states (DS) for each substation are stochastically sampled from lognormal fragility functions based on scenario-specific Intensity Measures (IM). Crucially, these physical damage states are mapped to functional restoration times rather than generic repair averages.


## P0026 / body block 26 | Front matter

Network Functionality & Robustness Analysis: The system’s serviceability is evaluated through a topology-based dependency matrix that links substations to census tracts. This module computes the functional fraction of power supply for each tract, simulating the grid's N-1 redundancy by allowing partial service delivery even when individual components fail.


## P0027 / body block 27 | Front matter

Resource-Constrained Recovery Simulation: Damaged substations are prioritized based on competing strategies (e.g., Centrality-first, Hospital-first, Impact-first). This module integrates a model that accounts for finite crew availability and travel trajectories along the real road network, dynamically updating system performance as repairs are completed.


## P0028 / body block 28 | Front matter

Equity-Aware Typology Analysis: Finally, a PCA and K-means clustering approach synthesizes intrinsic tract characteristics (social vulnerability) with simulation-derived outage and recovery metrics. This classification yields tract typologies that reveal spatial disparities in resilience, directly linking engineering performance to social consequences.


## P0029 / body block 29 | Front matter

The role of this framework lies in its hazard-consistent coupling of fragility to stochastic restoration times, the incorporation of route-constrained logistics within a real-world network, and the geographically explicit evaluation that closes the loop between infrastructure resilience and social equity.


## P0030 / body block 30 | Front matter

Methods and Results


## P0031 / body block 31 | Front matter

This study establishes a multi-layered computational framework to evaluate the seismic resilience of the Los Angeles power grid, integrating critical infrastructure topology, seismological hazard modeling, and socio-demographic vulnerability. The physical network model synthesizes substation geolocation data from OpenStreetMap with high-voltage transmission geometries from the California Energy Commission (CEC) to define a topologically valid graph. To capture spatially distributed hazards, we utilize USGS ShakeMaps to assign scenario-specific Peak Ground Acceleration (PGA) intensities to each network node. We examine three historical earthquake scenarios-the 1933 Long Beach, 1971 San Fernando, and 1994 Northridge events-selected to represent diverse seismotectonic sources and distinct spatial damage footprints ranging from regional disruptions to citywide blackouts.


## P0032 / body block 32 | Front matter

The societal dimension of these disruptions is assessed by overlaying the physical grid onto U.S. Census Bureau tract boundaries, populated with American Community Survey (ACS) demographic data and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) scores to quantify equity in recovery. Furthermore, human mobility dynamics are incorporated using Origin-Destination (OD) trip data from Replica, enabling the estimation of "flow loss" where travel is impeded by power outages.


## P0033 / body block 33 | Front matter

The simulation pipeline progresses through three analytical stages. First, stochastic fragility modeling maps local PGA values to discrete substation damage states via lognormal fragility functions, using Monte Carlo simulation to sample probabilistic failure modes and physical repair times. Second, network criticality analysis evaluates structural robustness using graph metrics (e.g., degree and betweenness) and impact-based measures quantifying the fragmentation of the Largest Connected Component (LCC). Finally, functional restoration is simulated under two regimes: a theoretical unconstrained baseline and a realistic, resource-constrained scheduling model involving 28 repair crews dispatched from 7 bases with explicit travel-time modeling (Los Angeles Department of Water and Power). Within this constrained environment, we evaluate the efficacy of four prioritization strategies-Random, Centrality-first, Impact-first (Population), and Hospital-first-aggregating component-level repairs into system-level recovery curves. The analysis concludes by classifying census tracts into resilience typologies using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and K-Means clustering, thereby identifying distinct vulnerability profiles based on recovery performance and socio-demographic characteristics.


## P0034 / body block 34 | Front matter

A. Data Loading & Weight Matrix Construction


## P0035 / body block 35 | Front matter

Data Preparation


## P0036 / body block 36 | Front matter

The foundation of the simulation is the integration of heterogeneous geospatial datasets. The physical power grid is represented by substation point inventories and high-voltage transmission line geometries synthesized from California Energy Commission (CEC) and Homeland Infrastructure Foundation-Level Data (HIFLD) sources. To characterize seismic hazards, we assign scenario-specific Peak Ground Acceleration (PGA) values to each substation by sampling spatially interpolated ShakeMap fields. Census tracts serve as the fundamental unit of analysis, represented by attributes including geometry, population, Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), National Risk Index (NRI) and hospital locations. The study area is set within the City of Los Angeles administrative boundaries.


## P0037 / body block 37 | Front matter

Topological Reconstruction of the Transmission Network


## P0038 / body block 38 | Front matter

Transforming raw GIS geometries into a mathematically valid network graph G is a critical prerequisite for subsequent analysis. MultiLineString of Transmission lines are first broken into LineString segments, whose endpoints are snapped to the nearest substation coordinates within a spatial tolerance of 300 m using a KD-tree index. Transmission lines passing through substations without explicit vertices are split at intersection points to ensure that all substations function as active nodes within the topology. An undirected weighted MultiGraph is then constructed using the vertices in LineStrings and edges connecting every consecutive vertex. This serves as a model representation of the real transmission graph. Substations are snapped to the nearest graph node. The substation-substation distances are then calculated by Dijkstra shortest path on graph G.


## P0039 / body block 39 | Front matter

The topological graph requires straight connection between each pair of directly connected substations, instead of the real geometry. A pair is considered directly adjacent only if the vertices listed in the shortest path are not associated with any other substation. An undirected edge is then constructed between directly adjacent pairs with the weight being the calculated along-grid shortest path.

C. Static Graph Criticality and Percolation


## P0085 / body block 86 | Front matter

Graph construction


## P0086 / body block 87 | Front matter

## P0118 / body block 119 | Front matter

Service-weighted criticality: population-based impact


## P0119 / body block 120 | Front matter

Beyond purely structural notions of node importance, a service-weighted impact metric that expresses connectivity degradation in terms of the population potentially affected is considered. This metric leverages the tract–substation coupling table to assign each substation an “attributed population” based on fractional service dependence. Specifically, for each mapping record linking tract t to substation i with weight wt,i and tract population popt, we define the population assignment to substation i as popt wt,i. ## P0165 / body block 166 | Front matter

E. Resource-Constrained Recovery and System Synthesis


## P0166 / body block 167 | Front matter

Resource-Constrained Dispatch Model


## P0167 / body block 168 | Front matter

We model post-earthquake restoration as a resource-constrained Multi-Depot Vehicle Routing and Scheduling Problem (MDVRSP). Let S be the set of all substations, and let J⊆S denote the subset classified as damaged in a given Monte Carlo realization. A finite workforce of K=28 repair crews is distributed across D=7maintenance depots. Each crew k∈{1,…,K} is anchored to a home depot d(k)∈D at initialization and executes a time-ordered sequence of repair tasks.


## P0168 / body block 169 | Front matter

State variables. At any decision epoch, each crew is characterized by its current location (either a depot or the most recently completed substation), and its next-available time. The system state is defined by the set of remaining unrepaired damaged substations and the crew states. Each damaged substation j∈J is a single indivisible task that can be served by exactly one crew; parallel work on a single substation is not modeled.


## P0169 / body block 170 | Front matter

Travel-time coupling. Spatial frictions are modeled through a precomputed impedance matrix τ(a,b) (hours) between any two locations a,b∈D∪S. These travel times are computed from shortest paths on a routable OpenStreetMap road graph, ensuring that dispatch decisions reflect the actual topology of the Los Angeles transportation network rather than straight-line distances. The travel-time matrix is treated as fixed for a given analysis run. It does not depend on time of day or post-event road degradation unless explicitly modeled elsewhere.


## P0170 / body block 171 | Front matter

Operational constraints. The formulation enforces (i) crew capacity: at most K concurrent repairs, one per crew; (ii) precedence in time: a crew cannot start a task until it has completed its prior task and traveled to the new site; and (iii) geographic feasibility: crews begin at their assigned depots and move along the road network, incurring travel time between consecutive tasks.


## P0171 / body block 172 | Front matter

Because the full MDVRSP is NP-hard at realistic network scales, we do not attempt an exact optimization. Instead, we evaluate a family of policy-driven greedy heuristics within a discrete-event simulation, which allows transparent comparisons among prioritization strategies while preserving the key physical constraints.


## P0172 / body block 173 | Front matter

Discrete-Event Simulation (DES) and Heuristic Policies


## P0173 / body block 174 | Front matter

Event Logic and Scheduling Timeline


## P0174 / body block 175 | Front matter

Recovery is simulated using a logistics-aware Discrete-Event Simulation (DES) that advances time by operational events rather than fixed time steps. Two event types are sufficient to define the restoration timeline: Crew availability (decision event) occurs when a crew becomes free to accept a new assignment at time t. Repair completion (state-change event) occurs when a substation transitions to the functional state at the instant its repair finishes.


## P0175 / body block 176 | Front matter

Initialization. At t=0, all crews are located at their home depots. The damaged task set J is read from the Monte Carlo realization, and each damaged substation is assigned a repair time sample consistent with its damage state.


## P0176 / body block 177 | Front matter

Dispatch and start time. When crew k becomes available at time t, the simulator selects the next task j⋆ from the remaining set Jrem⊆J. The crew then departs immediately, arriving at time t+τ(lk(t),j⋆), where lk(t) is the crew’s current location. Repair begins upon arrival, and the repair completes at


## P0177 / body block 178 | Front matter

tfinish=t+τ(lk(t),j⋆)+Rj⋆.


## P0178 / body block 179 | Front matter

At tfinish, the task is marked complete, the crew state is updated, and the substation’s functionality switches from failed to functional. The event queue is updated, and the simulator proceeds to the next earliest event.


## P0179 / body block 180 | Front matter

When multiple tasks share identical priority scores, ties are broken deterministically by smaller travel time, then by stable node ID to ensure reproducibility. Random policy uses a controlled pseudo-random generator with a fixed seed per realization to enable fair comparisons.


## P0180 / body block 181 | Front matter

## P0181 / body block 182 | Front matter

At each decision event, a heuristic policy π selects


## P0182 / body block 183 | Front matter

## P0183 / body block 184 | Front matter

## P0184 / body block 185 | Front matter

(1) Centrality-first (Impact Centrality). Prioritizes structurally important substations to accelerate restoration of connectivity. The score is derived from impact centrality. Conceptually, this policy targets substations whose restoration most rapidly improves the integrity of the network.


## P0185 / body block 186 | Front matter

(2) Impact-first (Population). Prioritizes substations expected to restore service to the largest number of people. The population impact of a substation can be computed by aggregating the tract dependency weights multiplied by tract population, producing a node-level “served population potential.” This makes the policy explicitly service-oriented rather than topology-oriented.


## P0186 / body block 187 | Front matter

(3) Hospital-first. Prioritizes substations that support healthcare access. Substations are ranked by their contribution to powering tracts containing the 104 hospitals in Los Angeles by December 2025 (from OpenStreetMap), ensuring that life-line services receive early restoration attention.


## P0187 / body block 188 | Front matter

(4) Random. Selects an unrepaired damaged substation uniformly at random and serves as a baseline representing uncoordinated recovery.


## P0188 / body block 189 | Front matter

Importantly, these policies differ only in task selection. All policies are evaluated under the same operational constraints, allowing a clear comparison of decision logic.

## P0381 / body block 382 | Front matter

Future Work and Conclusions


## P0382 / body block 383 | Front matter

This project presents a comprehensive pipeline to evaluate earthquake impacts on electric transmission-grid functionality and tract-level outcomes. A topological representation of the transmission network is constructed to support substation centrality and criticality analysis. For three earthquake scenarios, scenario-specific intensity fields are assigned to substations, fragility functions are applied to estimate probabilistic substation damage states, and a tract-substation mapping matrix propagates substation disruption into census-tract outage probabilities, producing spatially explicit impact maps. Recovery across scenarios is then simulated and compared using power-supply restoration and network-robustness metrics under both unconstrained and logistics-aware restoration conditions. The project concludes with principal component analysis (PCA) and k-means clustering to classify census tracts into resilience typologies based on simulated recovery outcomes and socio-economic attributes.


## P0383 / body block 384 | Front matter

Overall, the City of Los Angeles power grid forms a connected, highly clustered small-world network, so restoring a relatively small set of structurally important substations can rapidly improve systemwide connectivity. However, which substations count as “important” depends on the centrality metric used (e.g., degree vs. betweenness vs. impact), and these network-based priorities do not necessarily coincide with equity-priority considerations. In our simulation of the 1933 Long Beach, the 1971 San Fernando, and the 1994 Northridge earthquake scenarios, the Northridge creates the most damage and requires the longest time for reparation. Across scenarios, prioritizing the repair of substations with high Degree Centrality or Betweenness Centrality and those serving hospitals are shown to be the most efficient strategies for the system service recovery to 50%.


## P0384 / body block 385 | Front matter

Results suggest the following three restoration schemes targeting different objectives. For efficient restoration of the power-grid network, the City should prioritize substations with high degree to reconnect large portions of the network quickly or high betweenness to protect bridge nodes that maintain global connectivity. For efficient restoration of electricity service to the population, the City should prioritize substations that serve tracts containing hospitals, paired with high-degree substations for faster restoration of connectivity, as well as high-betweenness substations to preserve cross-area connectivity and prevent the grid from splitting into isolated components. For equitable restoration, the City should prioritize the central-west area identified as Cluster 0.


## P0385 / body block 386 | Front matter

The limitations of this study primarily arise from scope and model resolution. The analysis considers three historical earthquake scenarios, which limits generalization. The mapping from substation to census tract only considers the along-grid distance without detailed distribution line information. The logistics-aware restoration simulation uses predefined repair times and repair crew number while not addressing the elevated congestion after the earthquake event. The derived recovery time estimations and restoration strategy rankings may vary in alternative staffing levels and transportation conditions.


## P0386 / body block 387 | Front matter

Future work can expand scenario sampling and run sensitivity tests to track the influence of fragility, repair times, tract-substation assignment, and logistics inputs on time-to-50% service, robustness, hospital coverage, and equity gaps. The restoration model can be strengthened with repair task scheduling optimization, power system operational simulation, and post-earthquake road-damage-adjusted travel times.
