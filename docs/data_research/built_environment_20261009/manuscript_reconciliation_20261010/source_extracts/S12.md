Abstract
Major earthquakes can extensively disrupt electric-power systems, resulting in prolonged and
spatially uneven power-service losses within metropolitan regions. In metropolitan regions, post-
earthquake power recovery depends not only on which transmission and substation assets are
damaged, but also on how restoration resources are dispatched and which communities remain
without service. This study presents a census tract-level framework for evaluating post-
earthquake electric-power service disruption and recovery in the contiguous urban area of Los
Angeles County. The framework reconstructs a source-gated transmission topology, simulates
scenario-based substation damage using fragility functions, translates substation functionality
into census-tract service availability through tract-substation dependency weights, and
compares unconstrained recovery with logistics-aware restoration strategies, including rule-
based priorities and Genetic Algorithm (GA)-based scheduling recommendations. Using the 2%-
in-50-year seismic scenario as the primary case, the results show that network vulnerability is
concentrated in a limited set of high-impact substations and that tract-level recovery is spatially
uneven even before crew-dispatch constraints are introduced. Under logistics-aware restoration,
strategy performance cannot be explained by network centrality alone because population
exposure, tract-substation dependency, and repair sequencing jointly shape service recovery.
The hospital-prioritized rule provides the fastest recovery among the tested logistics-aware
strategies, while the population-impact rule provides a competitive non-optimization alternative
and GA-HospitalFirst performs best among the GA-based schedules. The recovery-vulnerability
typology further shows that delayed modeled restoration and social vulnerability are not
perfectly aligned. A composite hotspot analysis identifies the tracts where slow recovery, social
vulnerability, older housing stock, and disaster-risk exposure coincide. The proposed framework
supports socio-technical risk-informed restoration planning by comparing recovery priorities and
identifying communities where post-earthquake power-service delays may compound
vulnerabilities and exacerbate inequities.

1. Introduction
Electric systems serve as the backbone for the normal operation of society. As key components,
the functionality of substations and transmission lines is critical for the overall supply and
service of electricity. When earthquakes damage the electric-power system, neighborhoods can
experience different levels and durations of service disruption because local electricity access
depends on which substations remain functional, how transmission connectivity is affected, and
how quickly damaged assets can be restored. Post-earthquake power recovery is therefore not
only a system-performance problem, but also a community-service problem, as electricity
supports emergency response, healthcare operation and communication in affected areas.
Recent resilience research has emphasized that infrastructure recovery should not be evaluated
only through aggregate system functionality, because service losses and restoration delays are
often spatially uneven and socially differentiated.
1.1 Equity-aware infrastructure restoration
Recent infrastructure resilience studies have increasingly shifted from system-centered
performance metrics toward community-centered recovery outcomes. Logan and Guikema
(2020) reframed community resilience around equitable access to essential services,
emphasizing that recovery should be evaluated not only by infrastructure functionality, but also
by residents’ spatially uneven access to services such as food, health care, education, water,
power, sanitation, and communications. Dhakal and Zhang (2022) incorporated social equity
and vulnerability into infrastructure resilience assessment by accounting for unequal
distributions of functional loss and recovery time, thereby linking resilience metrics to both
inequality and impacts on socially vulnerable communities. Ding et al. (2026) extended this line
of work to the census-tract scale by examining the spatial-temporal dynamics of public
infrastructure service inequality during hurricanes using 311 service-request data, showing that
reported service requests and resolution time vary systematically across geographic exposure,
social vulnerability, and disaster-risk groups. In the power-outage context, Loni and Asadi (2024)
developed a data-driven index of social vulnerability to prolonged outages in California, while
Lin et al. (2022) framed equity as a missing dimension in power-grid resilience and argued that
recognitional, procedural, and distributional equity should be embedded into power-system
planning and disaster management. Esmalian et al. (2022) further showed, through a hazard-
household-infrastructure multi-agent model, that infrastructure service disruptions can impose
uneven household-level hardship, highlighting the need to evaluate recovery outcomes across
socially differentiated populations.
Beyond assessment, a related body of work has incorporated community characteristics into
restoration planning and resource-allocation models. Chen et al. (2025a) advanced community-
centered infrastructure restoration by combining population size, social vulnerability, and public
facilities, such as hospitals and emergency shelters, into restoration-priority weights for
interdependent power and water infrastructure recovery. Li et al. (2023) proposed a data-driven
and equity-centered framework for highway restoration following flood inundation, linking

Together, these studies show that a growing body of disaster recovery research has moved

methodological basis for evaluating restoration outcomes by asking not only how much
infrastructure functionality is restored, but also which communities regain service, when they
regain it, and how recovery benefits and burdens are distributed across space.
1.2 Substation Fragility, Repair Scheduling, Lifeline Restoration, and
Critical Service Restoration
A parallel stream of research has developed repair-scheduling, routing, and restoration-
optimization models for disrupted critical infrastructure systems. Xu et al. (2026) reviewed post-
disaster repair-resource scheduling models and showed that past research has matured around
restoration objectives, scheduling and routing decisions, operation constraints, uncertainty
treatment, and solution algorithms. Within this stream, Li et al. (2019) developed a joint
optimization model that simultaneously assigns restoration tasks to multiple teams, schedules
component repairs, and routes crews among geographically distributed damaged components
while evaluating recovery performance using maximal network flow and unmet demand.
Loggins et al. (2019) developed the CRISIS decision-support model to schedule repairs of
damaged civil infrastructure according to stakeholder-defined priorities for dependent social
infrastructure services, capturing interdependencies between civil systems and critical social
functions.
Related work has also expanded restoration scheduling beyond purely component-based repair
decisions. Shahverdi et al. (2025) formulated a decision-support model that coordinates
specialized repair crews across interdependent power, water, and transportation lifelines to
prioritize critical societal services such as hospitals. Shahverdi et al. (2024) further modeled
personnel as “human infrastructure,” emphasizing that critical services require not only
functional buildings and lifeline support but also skilled personnel who can remain in the
affected area and travel to their workplaces. Recent power-restoration models have also
addressed uncertainty-aware and adaptive decision-making: Jiang et al. (2025) proposed a
predict-then-optimize framework that combines repair-duration uncertainty prediction with
equity-constrained power restoration sequencing, and Li et al. (2026) developed a post-
earthquake electricity-gas restoration framework that jointly models damage-information
collection and repair-vehicle routing under partial observability.

For earthquake-specific power-system recovery, prior studies have coupled seismic damage,
network functionality, and restoration decisions at the system or component level. Xie et al.
(2024) developed a resilience-based optimisation framework for post-earthquake power-system
restoration by linking seismic-intensity attenuation, fragility-based component operational-state
assessment, graph-based system functionality metrics, and recovery-path optimisation. Fu et al.
(2024) advanced seismic vulnerability assessment of electrical substation systems by
combining hybrid equipment fragility functions, Bayesian updating with seismic damage-survey
data, and a Bayesian-network representation of substation topology and redundancy to infer
system functionality and component importance. These studies provide foundations for
modeling how earthquake-induced component damage affects power-system functionality and
recovery priorities.
Several studies are especially relevant to earthquake-induced electric-power risk and
restoration within Los Angeles County. Monte Carlo-based studies have linked earthquake
ground motion, component fragility, and electric transmission-network performance through
power-flow analysis. Shinozuka et al. (2007) evaluated seismic disruption of the Los Angeles
City power transmission network by sampling component damage under multiple earthquake
scenarios and assessing resulting network performance, while Johnson et al. (2020) similarly
used Monte Carlo simulation and power-flow analysis to estimate demand not served under
seismic damage. Other scenario-based work has emphasized the representation of regional
earthquake hazard and downstream outage consequences. Soleimani et al. (2021) developed a
Los Angeles City multi-hazard earthquake scenario selection method that preserves risk
estimates for ground shaking, liquefaction, and surface fault rupture while reducing
computational burden. Building on these scenarios, Cheng et al. (2024) quantified census-tract-
level electric-power risk in Los Angeles City by combining probabilistic earthquake and damage
scenarios, HAZUS-based component damage, DC load-flow analysis, and tract-level load-shed
allocation, showing that substation damage is a major driver of outage risk and that solar-plus-
storage benefits may remain spatially and socially uneven.
Other studies have modeled post-earthquake electric-power restoration processes in Los
Angeles more directly. Çağnan et al. (2006) provided an early City of Los Angeles-specific
benchmark for post-earthquake electric-power restoration by using discrete-event simulation to
project hazard-driven substation damage as restoration rapidity risk. Their results showed that
outage duration can vary geographically across Los Angeles Department of Water and Power
(LADWP) service areas and can be shaped as much by repair-team and material bottlenecks as
by initial component damage. Xu et al. (2007) further formulated a stochastic optimization model
for scheduling LADWP post-earthquake inspection, damage assessment, and repair tasks,
using restoration curves and outage-duration metrics to compare optimized schedules with
baseline or utility-informed restoration schedules. More broadly, logistics-oriented restoration
studies such as Cavdaroglu et al. (2013) show that repair sequencing, crew assignment, travel
time, precedence relationships, and repair uncertainty can substantially affect infrastructure
recovery trajectories.

Taken together, this body of work provides the engineering and logistical foundation for
modeling earthquake-induced power-system disruption and recovery. These studies have
advanced power-flow-based outage assessment, substation fragility modeling, repair scheduling
and logistics-aware recovery analysis. Their contributions show how damaged infrastructure
components, network constraints, resource limitations, and restoration operations shape power-
system recovery trajectories.
1.3 Focus of This Study
Despite these advances, a specific gap remains for earthquake-induced urban power-service
recovery: few studies perform a single metropolitan earthquake power-service recovery
assessment that links physical damage, restoration operations, and tract-level recovery
disparities. This gap limits the ability to evaluate not only how quickly the power system
recovers, but also how physical damage, restoration logistics, and prioritization rules translate
into uneven power-service recovery across census tracts and vulnerable communities.
Building on prior studies of earthquake-driven power-system risk, fragility-based component
damage, and resource-constrained restoration scheduling, this study asks three questions:
 How do earthquake-induced substation damage and source-gated transmission network
connectivity translate into census-tract-level power-service disruption and recovery
trajectories?
 How do utility yard logistics, crew constraints, and restoration prioritization strategies affect
population-weighted recovery and critical-service restoration?
 Which census tracts experience prolonged or vulnerability-amplified recovery, and how can
these outcomes be characterized through tract-level recovery-vulnerability typologies?
To address these questions, this study develops a tract-level assessment of post-earthquake
electric-power service disruption and recovery for the study area. The assessment couples
scenario-specific seismic intensity, substation fragility, source-gated transmission/substation
topology, tract-to-substation dependency mapping, damage-conditioned stochastic repair
durations, road-network travel from repair-yard origins, alternative restoration-prioritization
strategies, and consolidated metric-based tract typology identification. This framework builds on
earthquake-induced substation damage to model power-service recovery trajectories for
individual census tracts. Recovery outcomes are evaluated using tract-level service metrics,
including population-weighted recovery time and vulnerability-related recovery typologies
derived from social, demographic, built-environment, and simulated recovery indicators. A
sensitivity analysis further examines how assumptions on crew availability, repair-time scale,
Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) dependency threshold, and source-gate threshold affect
absolute recovery times and strategy-ranking stability. This study combines established hazard,
fragility, network-dependency, repair-logistics, and vulnerability-assessment components into a
transparent workflow for evaluating earthquake-specific power-service recovery and community-
level recovery disparities.

2. Methodology
Figure 1: Study Workflow. The framework consists of five core modules: (1) Topology and
Dependency Construction, which builds the source-gated transmission network and tract-
substation dependency mapping; (2) Seismic Damage Simulation, which estimates substation
damage states using fragility functions; (3) Damage-to-Service Translation, which translates
substation-level physical damage into tract-level service availability; (4) Recovery Modeling,
which simulates restoration trajectories under unconstrained baselines and logistics-aware
scheduling; and (5) Outputs and Interpretation, which evaluates population-weighted recovery
metrics and identifies recovery-vulnerability hotspots.
Figure 1 summarizes the workflow used to evaluate post-earthquake electric-power service
disruption and recovery in the study area. The workflow begins with five groups of input data:
transmission lines and substations, scenario Peak Ground Acceleration (PGA) fields, roads and
utility yards, hospitals, and tract-level attributes. These inputs are then processed through five
analytical modules: topology and dependency construction, seismic damage simulation,
damage-to-service translation, recovery modeling, and outputs interpretation. Together, the
workflow converts scenario-specific substation damage into tract-level service recovery
trajectories and recovery-vulnerability outcomes.
First, a transmission topology is constructed by combining substation point records with high-
voltage transmission-line geometries. The resulting graph is used to represent network
connectivity, identify source-connected components, and compute structural criticality measures
such as degree, betweenness, and connectivity-related impact on the largest connected

component. In parallel, a tract-to-substation dependency matrix is developed to allocate
substation functionality to census tracts using topology-informed spatial proximity.
Second, seismic hazard intensity is assigned to each substation from scenario PGA fields. The
analysis considers three historical earthquake scenarios, the 1933 Long Beach, 1971 San
Fernando, and 1994 Northridge events, together with a probabilistic 2%-in-50-year scenario.
Lognormal fragility functions are then used in a Monte Carlo procedure to sample discrete
substation damage states. These damage states are mapped to residual functionality levels and
damage-conditioned stochastic restoration durations.
Third, substation damage is translated into tract-level service disruption through a damage-to-
service propagation module. Residual substation functionality is routed through the source-
gated transmission topology and combined with tract-to-substation dependency weights to
estimate tract-level power-service availability.
Fourth, recovery modeling is conducted under both an unconstrained baseline and a logistics-
aware restoration setting. The unconstrained baseline characterizes tract-level recovery
trajectories based on damage-state restoration durations without crew-dispatch constraints. The
logistics-aware restoration model incorporates utility yard origins, road-network travel times, and
crew constraints. Within the constrained model, recovery outcomes are compared across rule-
based prioritization strategies and Genetic Algorithm (GA)-based optimization benchmarks to
evaluate how resource-constrained task sequencing affects tract-level service restoration.
Finally, recovery outcomes are interpreted using weighted recovery metrics, sensitivity analysis,
and recovery-vulnerability typologies. Population- and vulnerability-weighted recovery
trajectories are used to compare restoration strategies, while sensitivity analysis evaluates how
major modeling assumptions affect recovery time and strategy-ranking stability. Simulated
recovery indicators are then combined with social, demographic, built-environment, and hazard-
related tract attributes. These features are standardized and fed into K-means clustering to
identify census tracts where delayed recovery and vulnerability-related concerns coincide.
The pipeline integrates heterogeneous geospatial datasets. The physical power grid is
represented using substation inventories and high-voltage transmission-line geometries
obtained from the California Energy Commission (n.d.) and Homeland Infrastructure
Foundation-Level Data (n.d.). Scenario-specific ground-motion fields are obtained from U.S.
Geological Survey ShakeMap products (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.). Census tract boundaries
are obtained from U.S. Census Bureau TIGER/Line data (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b), with
demographic attributes joined from the American Community Survey (U.S. Census Bureau,
n.d.-a). Social Vulnerability Index attributes are obtained from the CDC/ATSDR Social
Vulnerability Index database (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention/Agency for Toxic
Substances and Disease Registry/Geospatial Research, Analysis, and Services Program, 2022),
National Risk Index (NRI) attributes from the FEMA National Risk Index dataset (Federal
Emergency Management Agency, n.d.), and hospital locations from California Health and
Human Services open data (California Health and Human Services Agency, n.d.).

2.1 Topological Network & Weight Matrix Construction
2.1.1 Topological Reconstruction of the Transmission Network
To transform raw power grid geometries into a network graph, transmission line records are first
decomposed into individual line-string components so that their vertices and segments can be
processed consistently. Then, to reconcile spatial offsets between the line and substation layers,
the endpoints of each line-string component are snapped to nearby substations. Endpoints
within 150 m of a substation are directly assigned to the nearest substation, whereas endpoints
within 150 to 300 m are assigned only when the nearest candidate is clearly distinguished from
the second-nearest candidate, based on a minimum distance margin of 75 m and a nearest-to-
second-nearest distance ratio not exceeding 0.75. To preserve existing line-line connectivity,
when a junction vertex cluster satisfies the snapping criteria, all vertices in the cluster are
moved together to the corresponding nearby substation. Nearby unresolved line endpoints were
merged within a 75 m tolerance as a conservative geometry-repair step.
Substations located near the interior of a line-string component are incorporated by splitting the
line at the substation’s orthogonal projection point when the substation-to-line distance is within
10 m. These splits are subject to compatibility checks based on line ownership and voltage
family, and a short connector representing the actual substation-to-line projection line is added
when necessary. The processed geometries are then converted into an undirected weighted

vertex pairs as length-weighted edges. For efficient distance calculations, each substation is

2.5.2 Rule-based Restoration Sequencing
Rule-based sequencing is primarily based on predefined priority criteria derived from substation-
level network and service metrics. We evaluate seven strategies:
(1) Impact-first. Prioritizes structurally important substations to accelerate restoration of network
connectivity. Substations are ranked by impact centrality first, betweenness centrality second,
and node degree third.
(2) Betweenness-first. Prioritizes substations that serve as bridging nodes in the transmission
graph. Substations are ranked by betweenness centrality first and node degree second.
(3) Degree-first. Prioritizes substation connected to large number of other substations.
Substations are ranked by node degree first and betweenness centrality second.
(4) Closeness-first. Prioritizes substations with short paths of connections to the rest of the
network. Substations are ranked by closeness centrality first and node degree second.
(5) Impact-first (Population). Prioritizes substations expected to restore service to the largest
number of people. Substations are ranked by population impact, computed as a node-level
population-importance proxy from tract dependency weights and tract population.
(6) Hospital-first. Prioritizes substations serving tracts that contain any hospital. Substations are
ranked first by the number of mapped hospital-containing census tract links and second by total
mapped population.
(7) Random. Generates a fixed-seed random order of damaged substations and serves as a
reproducible baseline representing uncoordinated recovery.
Importantly, these policies differ only in task selection. All policies are evaluated under the same
operational constraints, allowing a clear comparison of decision logic.

importance substations.

4. Discussion
4.1 Contextual Comparsion
Because a probabilistic Los Angeles earthquake restoration scenario cannot be directly
validated against observations, the modeled Northridge scenario is compared with historical
Northridge restoration evidence as a contextual scale check. Çağnan et al. (2006), citing
LADWP’s post-Northridge restoration record, reported that approximately 50%, 75%, 90%, 95%,
and 100% of LADWP utility customers were restored within 7, 11, 19, 26, and 168 h,
respectively. The present modeled Northridge scenario gives a population-weighted �80 of
14.25 h for the unconstrained baseline and 14.55-15.25 h across logistics-aware restoration
strategies. Since the historical record does not directly report �80 , linear interpolation between
the reported 75% and 90% customer-restoration milestones gives an approximate historical
value of 13.7 h. The modeled values are therefore close to the historical partial-restoration scale.
The comparison also shows that the model should not be treated as a replay of the 1994 event.
The modeled Northridge �50 is shorter than the historical 50% customer-restoration milestone,
while the modeled �90 and �95 are longer. This mismatch is expected because the present
framework does not model distribution feeders, customer-level switching, or utility emergency
operating records directly. Instead, it evaluates a controlled source-gated transmission service
proxy. The main value of the comparison is therefore confirmation that the modeled partial-
restoration scale is not inconsistent with the historical Northridge restoration record.
The mechanisms underlying the modeled Northridge scenario are also consistent with prior Los
Angeles restoration modeling. Çağnan et al. (2006) found that Los Angeles residents may
experience earthquake-induced power outages lasting up to about 10 days, that rapidity risk
varies geographically, and that restoration can be constrained by repair materials and
restoration-process limitations. Cheng et al. (2024) further showed that substation damage is
the key driver of census-tract-level load-shed risk in Los Angeles, while Xu et al. (2007) treated
post-earthquake LADWP restoration as a scheduling problem intended to reduce average
customer outage time. The present study extends these prior findings by translating substation
damage into time-dependent tract-level service recovery and by evaluating how source-gated
topology, tract-substation dependency, utility yard travel, crew constraints, and restoration
prioritization rules reshape recovery outcomes. The tested prioritization rules show a trade-off
between optimizing overall network connectivity and minimizing the immediate impact of power
outages on the population. While the network centrality-based rules allow for prompt
reconstruction of the physical grid, the rules informed by tract characteristics perform better in
shortening the outage duration for highly populated and vulnerable tracts.
4.2 Future Work
The present framework is intended as a transparent, scenario-based decision-support
assessment for comparing how hazard, network dependency, repair logistics, and prioritization


## PDF page 44 | Unassigned PDF section

44
assumptions shape tract-level recovery outcomes. Future work can extend the present
framework in several directions. First, the tract-level service representation can be refined by
incorporating distribution-network topology and utility customer-feeder connection data when
such data become available. This would allow transmission- and substation-level service
proxies to be linked more directly to feeder-level outage and restoration patterns. Second, the
tract-to-substation dependency model can be further improved by integrating utility service-
territory information, feeder maps, or empirical outage records, which would reduce reliance on
spatial proximity and topology-informed dependency assumptions.
The restoration component can also be expanded as more operational data become available.
Future studies could calibrate damage-conditioned restoration durations using utility repair
records, field inspection logs, or post-event restoration data. Road-network travel modeling
could also be enhanced by incorporating post-earthquake bridge damage, road closures,
congestion, emergency access restrictions, and time-varying travel conditions. In addition, the
restoration priority rules used here can be extended toward more realistic operational decision
models that account for utility-specific crew dispatch protocols, material availability, mutual-aid
coordination, and real-time damage information.
Further extensions can also strengthen the community-outcome interpretation. The recovery-
vulnerability typology developed in this study provides a descriptive classification of recurring
recovery profiles, and future work could examine the causal mechanisms linking infrastructure
dependency, social vulnerability, and delayed recovery. Hospital-service restoration can also be
refined by incorporating facility-level backup power, water supply, staffing, patient capacity, and
road accessibility, allowing future assessments to move from infrastructure-service access
toward a more comprehensive representation of hospital operational functionality.

5. Conclusion
This study presents an integrated tract-level assessment framework for post-earthquake
electric-power service recovery in the contiguous urban areas of Los Angeles County. By linking
seismic damage to substation assets, source-gated transmission network connectivity, tract-
substation service dependency, utility-yard and crew constraints, restoration-strategy
comparison, and recovery-vulnerability typologies, the framework connects physical
infrastructure disruption with community-level recovery outcomes. The analysis evaluates how
restoration decisions shape recovery trajectories and, under one representative strategy,
assesses how uneven recovery service availability and socio-economic characteristics interact
across census tracts, identifying areas where delayed restoration overlaps with higher social
vulnerability and disaster-risk exposure.
The results show that post-earthquake electric-power service recovery in the study area is
shaped by the interaction of network topology, tract-level dependency, repair logistics, and
social vulnerability. The source-gated topology concentrates network vulnerability in a limited
set of high-impact substations, while the unconstrained baseline shows that tract-level recovery
already varies spatially before crew-dispatch constraints are introduced. Under logistics-aware
restoration, utility yard locations, crew availability, travel time, and prioritization rules further
differentiate recovery outcomes.
The strategy comparison indicates that restoration priorities should be selected according to the
planning objective. Network centrality measures are useful for identifying substations that are
structurally important to transmission-network connectivity, but service-oriented strategies,
especially those based on population impact and hospital-related priority, minimize the durations
of service outages. GA-based strategies further show that milestone recovery, cumulative
recovery, and complete recovery can favor different restoration schedules and should therefore
serve as complementary performance metrics. Under the 2%-in-50-year scenario, the hospital-
first rule achieves the earliest population- and SVI-weighted �80 and the highest corresponding
AUC values among the logistics-aware strategies.
The recovery-vulnerability typology provides an additional community-level interpretation. The
clustering results show that delayed restoration and social vulnerability are not perfectly aligned.
Some socially vulnerable tracts recover relatively quickly in the modeled service network, while
some slower-recovery tracts are not among the most socially vulnerable. Therefore, the most
policy-relevant recovery-equity concern is concentrated in tracts where delayed restoration
overlaps with higher social vulnerability, older housing stock, and greater disaster-risk exposure.
The top recovery-vulnerability hotspots provide a more targeted screening output than either
recovery time or social vulnerability alone.
These findings address the study’s three core research questions. First, earthquake-induced
substation damage translates to tract-level disruption via the combined effects of spatially
varying ground motion, source-gated transmission network connectivity, and tract-substation
dependency, causing spatially uneven outages and differentiated layers of service importance

before repair logistics are considered. Second, utility-yard logistics, crew constraints, travel
times, and restoration priorities materially affect recovery. In the primary scenario, service-
oriented prioritization strategies, especially hospital-oriented choices, outperform network-
centrality rules in restoring power services to populations and critical hospital facilities. Finally,
clustering typologies and hotspots reveal that prolonged, vulnerability-amplified recovery occurs
in hotspot tracts around central Los Angeles County where delayed infrastructure restoration
overlaps directly with existing socioeconomic and disaster-risk burdens.
