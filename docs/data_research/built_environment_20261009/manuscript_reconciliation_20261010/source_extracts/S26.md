## P0004 / body block 5 | 2.1 Utility-compatible tract–substation mapping

2.1 Utility-compatible tract–substation mapping


## P0005 / body block 6 | 2.1 Utility-compatible tract–substation mapping

Main modification. The July mapping framework is retained. The revision changes the candidate set before distance-based weighting: strict SCE tracts use SCE-compatible stations, strict LADWP tracts use LADWP-compatible stations, and mixed or ambiguous tracts retain general eligibility.


## P0006 / body block 7 | 2.1 Utility-compatible tract–substation mapping

For tract r, the access station aᵣ is the geographically nearest station in the eligible set Eᵣ. The effective distance to candidate station s combines tract-to-access distance and shortest-path distance through the retained station network.


## P0007 / body block 8 | 2.1 Utility-compatible tract–substation mapping

aᵣ = arg minₛ∈Eᵣ d_geo(r,s)       dᵣₛ = d_geo(r,aᵣ) + d_net(aᵣ,s)


## P0008 / body block 9 | 2.1 Utility-compatible tract–substation mapping

The candidate weights remain inverse-square distance weights. They are normalized, weights below 3% are removed, and the retained weights are normalized again.


## P0009 / body block 10 | 2.1 Utility-compatible tract–substation mapping

qᵣₛ = 1(s∈Eᵣ)dᵣₛ⁻²       w̃ᵣₛ = qᵣₛ / Σⱼqᵣⱼ


## P0010 / body block 11 | 2.1 Utility-compatible tract–substation mapping

wᵣₛ = w̃ᵣₛ1(w̃ᵣₛ≥0.03) / Σⱼ[w̃ᵣⱼ1(w̃ᵣⱼ≥0.03)]


## P0011 / body block 12 | 2.1 Utility-compatible tract–substation mapping

The modification therefore changes which substations are allowed to serve as candidates; it does not replace the July network-distance, inverse-square weighting, or 3% sparsification logic.


## P0012 / body block 13 | 2.1 Utility-compatible tract–substation mapping

Reviewer concerns addressed. Reviewer 1 Comment 1: tract–substation dependency lacked utility/service-territory support and needed stronger robustness and clearer proxy interpretation.


## P0013 / body block 14 | 2.2 Realization-specific repair execution

2.2 Realization-specific repair execution


## P0014 / body block 15 | 2.2 Realization-specific repair execution

Main modification. The sampled damage state and sampled damage-conditioned repair time now remain attached to each physical realization through the actual crew schedule. The July use of representative repair durations for scheduling is removed.


## P0015 / body block 16 | 2.2 Realization-specific repair execution

For realization m, every station with DS > 0 becomes a repair task. The policy π remains a fixed priority sequence; undamaged stations are removed from that sequence without re-ranking the remaining tasks.


## P0016 / body block 17 | 2.2 Realization-specific repair execution

D⁽ᵐ⁾ = {i : DSᵢ⁽ᵐ⁾ > 0}       Tᵢ⁽ᵐ⁾ ~ G_DSᵢ⁽ᵐ⁾       π⁽ᵐ⁾ = filter(π,D⁽ᵐ⁾)


## P0017 / body block 18 | 2.2 Realization-specific repair execution

For the crew assigned to task i, start time equals the crew’s next available time plus directed travel from its current location. Completion time is start time plus the realized repair duration.


## P0018 / body block 19 | 2.2 Realization-specific repair execution

tᵢ,start⁽ᵐ⁾ = aₖ + τ(ℓₖ,i)       cᵢ⁽ᵐ⁾ = tᵢ,start⁽ᵐ⁾ + Tᵢ⁽ᵐ⁾


## P0019 / body block 20 | 2.2 Realization-specific repair execution

At completion, the station’s raw functionality returns to one and the crew becomes available at that station. No second recovery-time clock is started after completion.


## P0020 / body block 21 | 2.2 Realization-specific repair execution

fᵢ⁽ᵐ⁾(t) = f_DSᵢ⁽ᵐ⁾ + [1 − f_DSᵢ⁽ᵐ⁾]1(t ≥ cᵢ⁽ᵐ⁾)


## P0021 / body block 22 | 2.2 Realization-specific repair execution

The same realization-specific damage, repair times, road travel, and crew resources are used for every strategy. The strategy comparison therefore changes the repair order while holding the physical realization fixed.


## P0022 / body block 23 | 2.2 Realization-specific repair execution

Reviewer concerns addressed. Reviewer 1 Comment 3: realization-specific task sets and repair-time uncertainty were not carried clearly through scheduling. Reviewer 2 Comment 13: the meaning of logistics/resource constraints was not sufficiently clear.


## P0023 / body block 24 | 2.3 Source-connected service, loss components, and source-route condition

2.3 Source-connected service, loss components, and source-route condition


## P0024 / body block 25 | 2.3 Source-connected service, loss components, and source-route condition

Main modification. The production service rule still requires a functional station to have a path to an active Core source. The revision adds two pieces of information around that rule: it separates the modeled deficit by cause, and it records the source-route condition inside states that the binary gate simply labels “connected.”


## P0025 / body block 26 | Station service and loss components

Station service and loss components


## P0026 / body block 27 | Station service and loss components

A station first has a raw damage-dependent functionality fᵢ(t). It is counted as functional for network connectivity when fᵢ(t) ≥ 0.5. Cᵢ(t) equals one when that functional station can reach at least one active Core source through the current functional graph.


## P0027 / body block 28 | Station service and loss components

Fᵢ(t) = 1{fᵢ(t) ≥ 0.5}       Cᵢ(t) = 1{i → S_active(t)}       eᵢ(t) = fᵢ(t)Fᵢ(t)Cᵢ(t)


## P0028 / body block 29 | Station service and loss components

The total station-level deficit is then separated exactly into local damage, the additional functional-threshold loss, and loss caused by having no active-source path.


## P0029 / body block 30 | Station service and loss components

Lᵢ,self = 1 − fᵢ       Lᵢ,threshold = fᵢ(1 − Fᵢ)       Lᵢ,source = fᵢFᵢ(1 − Cᵢ)


## P0030 / body block 31 | Station service and loss components

1 − eᵢ = Lᵢ,self + Lᵢ,threshold + Lᵢ,source


## P0031 / body block 32 | Tract service and cumulative burden

Tract service and cumulative burden


## P0032 / body block 33 | Tract service and cumulative burden

Tract service is the weighted sum of effective service from its mapped stations. In the production mapping, the weights for every tract sum to one.


## P0033 / body block 34 | Tract service and cumulative burden

Aᵣ⁽ᵐ⁾(t) = Σᵢ wᵣᵢeᵢ⁽ᵐ⁾(t)


## P0034 / body block 35 | Tract service and cumulative burden

Cumulative burden is the service deficit integrated over the common 480 h horizon. Because station states change only at event times, the integral is evaluated exactly as the post-event deficit multiplied by the time to the next event.


## P0035 / body block 36 | Tract service and cumulative burden

bᵣ⁽ᵐ⁾ = ∫₀ᴴ[1 − Aᵣ⁽ᵐ⁾(t)]dt = Σₖ[1 − Aᵣ⁽ᵐ⁾(tₖ)](tₖ₊₁ − tₖ),    H = 480 h


## P0036 / body block 37 | Tract service and cumulative burden

The same mapping and event intervals are applied separately to Lself, Lthreshold, and Lsource, so the tract-level cumulative burden can be attributed to the same three causes.


## P0037 / body block 38 | Source-route condition inside the connected state

Source-route condition inside the connected state


## P0038 / body block 39 | Source-route condition inside the connected state

The binary gate only distinguishes “no active-source route” from “at least one active-source route.” The added network analysis records two additional quantities for every changing functional graph: the number of reachable active sources and the number of independent station routes from a station to active sources.


## P0039 / body block 40 | Source-route condition inside the connected state

Nᵢ,src(t) = |{s ∈ S_active(t) : i → s}|       κᵢ(t) = number of independent station routes to active sources


## P0040 / body block 41 | Source-route condition inside the connected state

For interpretation, a non-source station with κᵢ = 1 is one-route connected, while κᵢ ≥ 2 is multiple-route connected; an active Core source is identified separately. These route classes do not assign a different service value. Instead, the existing tract service can be separated according to which connection type supports it:


## P0041 / body block 42 | Source-route condition inside the connected state

Aᵣ(t) = Aᵣ,source(t) + Aᵣ,one-route(t) + Aᵣ,multiple-route(t)


## P0042 / body block 43 | Source-route condition inside the connected state

This links the path analysis directly to the reported service trajectories: total modeled service is unchanged, while the analysis shows whether that service is being supported by a local source, a single independent route, or multiple independent routes. The route composition can then be compared across restoration strategies and across tracts.


## P0043 / body block 44 | Source-route condition inside the connected state

Reviewer concerns addressed. Reviewer 1 Comment 2: a binary source-connected gate does not distinguish the condition or redundancy of connected states and is not a power-flow model. Reviewer 2 Comments 6–7: network criticality/percolation needed a clearer connection to the paper’s recovery question.


## P0044 / body block 45 | 2.4 Community-burden GA objective

2.4 Community-burden GA objective


## P0045 / body block 46 | 2.4 Community-burden GA objective

Main modification. The GA candidate sequence is now evaluated with the same tract-level cumulative service burden used in the reported recovery results, rather than the July station-level weighted completion score.


## P0046 / body block 47 | 2.4 Community-burden GA objective

For each candidate fixed sequence π, the evaluator runs the realization-specific task filter and crew schedule, updates station functionality and source connection, maps effective service to tracts, and integrates each tract’s burden. Population-weighted burden for realization m is:


## P0047 / body block 48 | 2.4 Community-burden GA objective

B⁽ᵐ⁾(π) = [ΣᵣPᵣbᵣ⁽ᵐ⁾(π)] / [ΣᵣPᵣ]


## P0048 / body block 49 | 2.4 Community-burden GA objective

The planning objective is the mean burden over 64 independent 2pc50 planning realizations; fitness is its negative because the GA is written as a maximization problem.


## P0049 / body block 50 | 2.4 Community-burden GA objective

B_plan(π) = (1/64)Σₘ₌₁⁶⁴B⁽ᵐ⁾(π)       J(π) = −B_plan(π)


## P0050 / body block 51 | 2.4 Community-burden GA objective

The search uses population 100, 100 generations, crossover 0.8, mutation 0.2, tournament size 3, and five seeds. The seven fixed rule-based sequences are evaluated on the same 64 planning realizations before the search. No GA seed improved the Impact-first incumbent, so Impact-first appears once in the formal strategy results.


## P0051 / body block 52 | 2.4 Community-burden GA objective

Reviewer concerns addressed. Reviewer 1 Comments 6–7: GA reproducibility, objective definition, and the earlier mismatch between GA fitness and reported recovery metrics. Reviewer 2 Comment 9: the GA was presented as a black box.


## P0052 / body block 53 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

2.5 Distributional recovery measures


## P0053 / body block 54 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

Main modification. The revision no longer uses one population-and-vulnerability-weighted recovery curve as the main evidence about distributional outcomes. It reports absolute burden by vulnerability group, group separation, inequality across tracts, and paired tract-level policy effects.


## P0054 / body block 55 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

Using the existing NRI-derived SOVI score, tracts are assigned once to four equal-count groups; Q4 is the highest-vulnerability quartile. For each realization, the population-weighted burden of group q is:


## P0055 / body block 56 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

B_q⁽ᵐ⁾ = [Σᵣ∈Qq Pᵣbᵣ⁽ᵐ⁾] / [Σᵣ∈Qq Pᵣ]       B_q = (1/N)ΣₘB_q⁽ᵐ⁾


## P0056 / body block 57 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

The Q4–Q1 comparison is reported both with sign and as an absolute separation. The absolute measure is calculated within each realization before averaging, so it is not the absolute value of the mean signed difference.


## P0057 / body block 58 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

Δ₄₁ = (1/N)Σₘ[B₄⁽ᵐ⁾ − B₁⁽ᵐ⁾]       D₄₁ = (1/N)Σₘ|B₄⁽ᵐ⁾ − B₁⁽ᵐ⁾|


## P0058 / body block 59 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

A population-weighted Gini provides a tract-wide inequality measure for each realization, and the reported value is the mean across realizations.


## P0059 / body block 60 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

μ⁽ᵐ⁾ = [ΣᵣPᵣbᵣ⁽ᵐ⁾]/[ΣᵣPᵣ]


## P0060 / body block 61 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

G⁽ᵐ⁾ = [ΣᵣΣⱼPᵣPⱼ|bᵣ⁽ᵐ⁾ − bⱼ⁽ᵐ⁾|] / [2μ⁽ᵐ⁾(ΣᵣPᵣ)²]       G = (1/N)ΣₘG⁽ᵐ⁾


## P0061 / body block 62 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

For a candidate policy p relative to reference policy p₀, each tract also receives a mean paired burden change. Negative values mean lower burden under the candidate policy; positive values mean higher burden.


## P0062 / body block 63 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

δᵣ = (1/N)Σₘ[bᵣ⁽ᵐ⁾(p) − bᵣ⁽ᵐ⁾(p₀)]


## P0063 / body block 64 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

Improved: δᵣ < −1 h       Near-zero: |δᵣ| ≤ 1 h       Worsened: δᵣ > 1 h


## P0064 / body block 65 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

Population is summed within the three tract classes. The ±1 h boundary is a practical reporting threshold; paired realization bootstrap intervals are reported separately for uncertainty in policy effects.


## P0065 / body block 66 | 2.5 Distributional recovery measures

Reviewer concerns addressed. Reviewer 1 Comment 5: SVI-weighted T80 was not an equity measure and typologies/hotspots should remain descriptive. Reviewer 2 Comments 10 and 14–16: vulnerability needed a clearer role, including who benefits, who is delayed, and the system-level cost of targeted restoration.


## P0066 / body block 67 | 2.6 Vulnerability-targeted repair priority

2.6 Vulnerability-targeted repair priority


## P0067 / body block 68 | 2.6 Vulnerability-targeted repair priority

Main modification. A new fixed rule directly prioritizes stations according to the high-vulnerability population mapped to them. It is a transparent rule-based comparator, not a second optimization problem.


## P0068 / body block 69 | 2.6 Vulnerability-targeted repair priority

For station s, the Q4 score is the mapped population from tracts in the highest-vulnerability quartile. Total mapped population is used only as the first tie-breaker, followed by station ID.


## P0069 / body block 70 | 2.6 Vulnerability-targeted repair priority

Vₛ = Σᵣ∈Q4 Pᵣwᵣₛ       Pₛ,mapped = ΣᵣPᵣwᵣₛ


## P0070 / body block 71 | 2.6 Vulnerability-targeted repair priority

Stations are sorted by descending Vₛ, then descending Pₛ,mapped, then ascending station ID. The resulting sequence is fixed before evaluation and is tested on the same physical realizations as the other repair priorities.


## P0071 / body block 72 | 2.6 Vulnerability-targeted repair priority

Reviewer concerns addressed. Reviewer 2 Comment 14: the paper needed an explicit equity-informed targeted restoration comparison that quantifies the benefit to high-vulnerability communities, the cost to other objectives, and who is affected. It also supports Reviewer 1 Comment 5 and Reviewer 2 Comment 10 by giving vulnerability a direct, measurable role in the strategy comparison.
